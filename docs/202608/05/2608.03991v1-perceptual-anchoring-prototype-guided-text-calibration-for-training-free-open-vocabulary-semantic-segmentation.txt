Abstract 

Training-free open-vocabulary semantic segmentation (OVSS) partitions an image into semantically distinct regions based on arbitrary text descriptions, without learning any additional parameters. However, existing methods typically focus on improving visual representations while treating text embeddings that encode only generic category concepts as fixed classification references. The resulting semantic gap between these generic concepts and the visual representations that capture the specific appearances of target instances often causes incomplete masks and erroneous predictions in non-target regions. Inspired by the symbol-percept correspondence underlying perceptual anchoring, we propose Prototype-Guided Text Calibration (PTC) for training-free OVSS. In the Perceiving stage, PTC selects reliable visual evidence based on initial matching scores to construct category-specific visual prototypes. In the Anchoring stage, PTC uses these prototypes to calibrate their corresponding text embeddings, with the calibration strength adaptively adjusted based on the amount of visual evidence. Consequently, the calibrated text embeddings align more accurately with instance-specific visual representations while preserving generic category semantics and open-vocabulary generalization. Moreover, PTC requires neither additional training nor external models and can serve as a plug-and-play module for existing methods. Extensive experiments across eight benchmarks show that PTC significantly enhances the performance of six representative methods and yields more complete and accurate segmentation results. These results validate PTC as a simple and e ff ective approach to improving visual-text alignment. The code is available at [https://github.com/Valeria-MaW/PTC ]. 

Keywords: Open-vocabulary, Semantic segmentation, Vision-language models, CLIP, Training-free 

1. Introduction 

Open-vocabulary semantic segmentation (OVSS) aims to partition an image into semantically distinct regions using arbitrary text descriptions. Unlike traditional semantic segmentation methods that rely on closed-set categories and costly dense annotations [ 1 , 2, 3, 4 ], OVSS can recognize classes unseen during training, making it better suited to real-world visual perception, which encompasses tens of thousands of categories. Large-scale vision-language pre-trained models (VLMs) (e.g., CLIP [ 5] and ALIGN [ 6]) have demonstrated impressive transferability in recognizing novel classes. This prompts researchers to apply them to OVSS and to explore their adaptation to dense prediction tasks [ 7 , 8, 9, 10 , 11 , 12 ], since CLIP is inherently designed for image-level visual-text matching. Existing OVSS methods can be broadly divided into training-based and training-free approaches, depending on whether additional parameter learning is required. Training-based approaches typically rely on large segmentation datasets [ 7, 8, 13 , 14 ], image-text pair datasets [ 9, 10 , 15 ], or other forms of supervision [ 16 , 17 ], introducing significant computational costs. In contrast, training-free OVSS has attracted increasing attention because it requires no additional parameter learning and facilitates generalization to new scenes and classes. Most existing training-free methods focus on improving visual representations, primarily by enhancing their local perception and spatial consistency, thereby improving visual–text matching and segmentation performance

However, these methods often treat class text embeddings as reliable zero-shot classifiers and directly match them to visual features, which overlooks a semantic gap between category text embeddings and visual representations. Specifically, class text embeddings generated by pre-trained large language models (LLMs) [ 29 , 30 ] learn generic, input-agnostic category concepts. In contrast, visual representations are extracted from the input image and characterize the specific appearances of target instances under particular poses, viewpoints, or imaging conditions. Consequently, directly matching generic category text embeddings with instance-specific visual representations inevitably su ff ers from semantic misalignment, which naturally degrades segmentation performance and manifests in two limitations. First, the model tends to recognize only local regions that are highly representative of the category, resulting in incomplete mask coverage, as shown in the upper part of Fig. 1(a). Second, generic text embeddings may be incorrectly matched with visually similar background regions or with regions belonging to co-occurring classes, resulting in erroneous segmentation, as shown in the lower part of Fig. 1(a). Since final segmentation predictions inherently rely on visual–text matching, refining only visual representations while leaving generic text embeddings unchanged fails to fully mitigate the aforementioned semantic gap. To bridge this gap, we draw inspiration from perceptual anchoring in cognitive robotics, a conceptual mechanism that links abstract symbols to concrete percepts, allowing the symbols to refer to specific entities [ 31 , 32 ]. From this perspective, we argue that category text embeddings should not always be treated as fixed classification references. Instead, they should be linked to the instance-specific visual representations in the input image using reliable perceptual evidence, enabling these abstract category concepts to more accurately refer to the target instances, thus achieving more precise segmentation. Building on this insight, we propose Prototype-Guided Text Calibration (PTC) for training-free OVSS. In the Perceiving stage, PTC conducts a reliability assessment and an evidence-quantity strategy to mitigate noise interference and to accommodate target-scale variations, thereby selecting reliable visual evidence based on the initial matching scores to construct category prototypes. In the Anchoring stage, PTC uses these prototypes to calibrate the corresponding text embeddings and adaptively adjusts the calibration strength based on evidence su ffi ciency to prevent semantic deviations caused by over-reliance on limited evidence. In this way, we establish a link between generic category concepts and category instances with specific appearances, which enables calibrated text embeddings to preserve generic semantics while aligning more closely with instance-specific visual representations, thereby improving final segmentation performance. The main contributions of this work are as follows: 2• We identify an underexplored semantic gap in training-free OVSS between generic category text embeddings and instance-specific visual representations derived from the input, which limits the accuracy of visual-text alignment and thereby constrains segmentation performance. 

• We propose PTC, a novel prototype-guided text calibration method that can be integrated into various training-free OVSS methods as a plug-and-play module. It calibrates category text embeddings using visual prototypes with evidence-driven adaptive calibration, aligning them more closely with visual representations while preserving generic semantics, thereby improving visual–text matching. 

• We conduct extensive experiments on eight segmentation benchmarks and integrate PTC into six representative training-free OVSS baselines. Quantitative and qualitative results validate the e ff ectiveness of PTC. 

2. Related Work 

Visual representation enhancement . Most existing training-free OVSS methods directly modify the internal architec-ture of CLIP, enabling its visual representations to capture local information. MaskCLIP [18] modifies the final-layer self-attention to exploit CLIP’s latent localization capability. SCLIP [ 11 ] replaces this with correlative self-attention to enhance spatial consistency of visual representations. NACLIP [ 19 ] extends this by strengthening local neighborhood modeling to improve the continuity of predictions across neighboring regions. CLIP Surgery [ 20 ], GEM [ 21 ], and CLIPtrase [ 22 ] introduce more flexible and generalized formulations of self-attention, thereby enhancing segmentation performance. ClearCLIP [ 23 ] removes the residual branch and feed-forward network from the final layer to obtain cleaner dense representations. ResCLIP [ 24 ] exploits localization information in intermediate-layer attention to improve category consistency. Moreover, other studies leverage spatial information from external models to compensate for CLIP’s limited local perception. ProxyCLIP [ 12 ] constructs proxy attention from spatial correspondences between vision foundation models (VFMs), e.g., SAM [ 26 ], DINO [ 27 ], DINOv2 [ 33 ], and CLIP features to enhance CLIP’s local consistency. CorrCLIP [ 25 ] uses SAM to constrain patch interactions and reconstructs more coherent patch corre-lations by self-supervised vision models, thereby improving region predictions. For high-resolution images, Trident [28 ] proposes a splice-then-segment paradigm, which first splices CLIP and DINO features from sub-images, then uses the global correlations provided by SAM to aggregate features and refine predictions. Despite these advancements, these methods typically treat category text embeddings as fixed classification references and overlook the inherent semantic gap between generic category text embeddings and the instance-specific visual representations in the input, leading to limited segmentation performance. 

Visual reference construction . Recent e ff orts use external models to construct visual references and segment target objects based on robust correspondences in the visual representation space. FreeDA [ 34 ] constructs visual references from images generated by Stable Di ff usion [ 35 ] and performs prediction by combining local region-to-prototype similarity with global image-to-text similarity. FOSSIL [ 36 ] constructs retrievable synthetic visual references and uses OpenCut to generate candidate regions from input images, then matches their embeddings with prototypes in a self-supervised visual space. Moreover, RIM [ 37 ] uses Stable Di ff usion [ 35 ] and SAM to construct image references and to perform image-to-image matching via relation-aware ranking in the DINOv2 feature space. Rather than directly improving visual features, these methods construct category-level visual references for visual-visual matching, alleviating the ambiguity and noise caused by the modality gap during visual-text matching. Notably, these references represent generic visual patterns for each category as they are externally constructed from other pre-trained models. Consequently, these methods still essentially match generic semantics with instance-specific representations, and while useful for target localization, they struggle to accurately align object boundaries for dense prediction. Instead, PTC directly constructs visual prototypes from reliable visual evidence in the current input, which are not used to replace text embeddings but to calibrate the original category text embeddings, aiming to establish a perceptual link between generic concepts and the specific visual appearances of the corresponding instances. This enables the calibrated text embeddings to align more closely in semantics with instance-specific visual representations, thereby mitigating the semantic gap. 3Text-side adaptation . Several studies explore how category text descriptions a ff ect training-free OVSS. LLM-Supervision [ 38 ] aggregates predictions from GPT-3 [ 39 ]-generated subclass descriptions to represent the segmentation mask of the target category, whereas ITACLIP [ 40 ] directly fuses text embeddings of category synonyms for prediction. Moreover, FreeCP [ 41 ] purifies the candidate category set to reduce the e ff ects of category redundancy and confusion on segmentation results. Although the first two methods enrich category descriptions, they still rely on fixed, pre-generated text that encodes only generic semantics. Meanwhile, FreeCP primarily focuses on filtering candidate classes to mitigate confusion. Consequently, the semantic gap between generic category text embeddings and instance-specific visual representations, along with the resulting limited segmentation performance, remains underexplored.

3.2.2. Perceiving: Reliable Visual Prototype Construction 

In the Perceiving stage, PTC extracts reliable visual evidence from visual features and aggregates it into class-specific visual prototypes that characterise the appearance of the category instance in the current image. This stage inherently faces two key challenges. First, the initial matching scores are susceptible to background noise and category confusion, making it essential to evaluate the reliability of candidate tokens to construct high-quality visual prototypes. Second, it is challenging to adapt the number of evidence tokens to variations in object scale, which requires a careful balance between ensuring su ffi cient tokens to guarantee prototype representativeness and avoiding the oversampling of noisy or ambiguous tokens that could contaminate the prototype. To overcome these challenges, PTC measures token reliability using the score margin and employs a hybrid strategy to determine the number of evidence tokens required for each category. 4. Experiments 

4.1. Implementation Details 

Datasets . We conduct a comprehensive evaluation on eight well-established datasets and categorize these datasets into two groups: (1) with the background class : Pascal VOC21 (VOC21) [ 43 ], PASCAL Context60 (Context60) [ 44 ], COCO-Object (Object) [ 45 ]; (2) without the background class : Pascal VOC20 (VOC20) [ 43 ], Cityscapes (City) [46 ], Pascal Context59 (Context59) [ 44 ], ADE20k-150 (ADE) [ 47 ], COCO-Stu ff (Stu ff ) [ 45 ]. All experiments strictly followed the original dataset division guidelines to ensure the reliability of the assessment results. 

Implementation & metric . Our implementations rely on MMSegmentation [ 53 ] and MMEngine [ 54 ]. For image preprocessing, we resize the shorter side of each image to 336 pixels for the PASCAL and COCO datasets, and to 448 pixels for the higher-resolution Cityscapes and ADE20K datasets. For each input image, PTC extracts reliable visual evidence from overlapping 224 × 224 local crops with a stride of 112 × 112 and aggregates it across all crops to construct category visual prototypes, which are used to calibrate the corresponding text embeddings only once for the entire image. Final segmentation is performed using a sliding window inference strategy with a 336 ×336 window and a 112 ×112 stride. Following prior work [ 11 , 12 ], we obtain class text embeddings using the standard ImageNet prompts [ 5] and the corresponding class names. Our method undergoes direct evaluation on the validation set of all datasets without the need of retraining or fine-tuning. The semantic segmentation performance is evaluated by using mean intersection over union (mIoU). Meanwhile, any post-processing (e.g., DenseCRF [ 55 ] or Pixel-Adaptive Mask Refinement (PAMR) [ 56 ]) is refused in our results. 4.2. Comparison Results 

Compared baselines . The compared methods are classified into two groups. The first group consists of methods that rely solely on CLIP, including ReCo [ 48 ], MaskCLIP [ 18 ], CaR [ 49 ], GEM [ 21 ], CLIPtrase [ 22 ], OVDi ff 

[ 50 ], SCLIP [ 11 ], ClearCLIP [ 23 ], NACLIP [ 19 ], and ResCLIP [ 24 ]. The second group comprises methods that incorporate additional models, including ZeroGuideSeg [ 51 ], RIM [ 37 ], FreeDA [ 34 ], LLM-Supervision [ 38 ], LaVG [52], ProxyCLIP [12], and CorrCLIP [25]. Results are presented in Table 1. 

Quantitative results . As a plug-and-play text calibration method, PTC can be readily integrated into existing training-free OVSS methods to alleviate the semantic gap between generic category text embeddings and instance-specific visual representations. As shown in Table 1, applying PTC to SCLIP, ClearCLIP, NACLIP, ResCLIP, ProxyCLIP, and CorrCLIP improves their average mIoU by 1.5, 1.0, 2.2, 0.8, 1.9, and 0.9 percentage points, respectively. Notably, PTC not only substantially improves baselines with relatively limited initial performance, increasing the average mIoU of NACLIP from 39.0% to 41.2%, but also delivers further gains on strong baselines such as CorrCLIP, raising its average mIoU from 51.0% to 51.9%. These results collectively demonstrate the e ff ectiveness and generalization of PTC.

Eff ect of Reliability Assessment Criteria . To evaluate the proposed margin-based reliability assessment, we compare three criteria: confidence thresholding, confidence-based top-Kc ranking, and margin-based top-Kc ranking. For both ranking-based criteria, the number of evidence tokens for each category, Kc, is determined by the hybrid evidence-quantity strategy of PTC, as defined in Eq. (6). Instead, confidence thresholding retains all visual tokens with confidence scores above τ. The results are reported in Table 2. As shown in Table 2, confidence thresholding exhibits inconsistent performance across six representative bench-marks. At τ = 0.35, it improves upon the baseline only on PASCAL VOC21 and COCO-Object, while degrading performance on the remaining datasets, with particularly large drops on Cityscapes, ADE20K-150, and COCO-Stu ff . In addition, further increasing the confidence threshold does not yield consistent improvements. These results indicate that relying solely on a fixed confidence threshold fails to robustly select reliable visual evidence across di ff erent categories. A low threshold tends to include ambiguous tokens, thereby contaminating visual prototypes, whereas a high threshold may leave some categories with insu ffi cient visual evidence, thereby weakening the overall e ff ectiveness of PTC. Compared to the fixed confidence threshold, both top-Kc selection strategies are more stable and outperform this criterion on all datasets. Moreover, a closer comparison reveals a slight overall advantage for margin-based top-Kc.Specifically, its performance matches confidence-based top-Kc on PASCAL Context60, COCO-Object, and ADE20K, improves results on PASCAL VOC21 and COCO-Stu ff by 0.1%, and is 0.1% lower on Cityscapes alone. Although the performance di ff erence is small, the score margin accounts for the strongest competing category and therefore provides a more direct measure of category-assignment ambiguity than absolute confidence. Accordingly, we adopt margin-based top-Kc ranking as the default reliability assessment criterion.

as the target evidence count for each category, whereas proportional sampling selects ⌊ρNc⌋ tokens according to the size of the corresponding candidate set. By assigning the same evidence count regardless of candidate-set size, it provides insu ffi cient coverage for large candidate sets while increasing the inclusion of unreliable tokens in small or noisy candidate sets, thereby limiting the representativeness and reliability of visual prototypes. By contrast, proportional sampling adjusts the target evidence count according to the size of each category’s candidate set, but its performance is highly sensitive to ρ. 4.3.2. Sensitivity Analysis of K min and μ

In this subsection, we analyze the e ff ects of the hyperparameters Kmin and μ on segmentation performance. Kmin 

specifies the minimum quantity of visual evidence required for a category to participate in prototype construction and text calibration. μ is the global calibration strength, which controls the overall influence of the visual prototype on the original category text embedding. To examine their individual e ff ects, we vary one parameter at a time while fixing the other at its dataset-specific default value. The results on four representative datasets are shown in Fig. 5. Overall, segmentation performance initially improves, then saturates or declines as either Kmin or μ increases. Within this trend, datasets favor di ff erent optimal values due to di ff erences in vocabulary size, the number of categories present in each image, object scales, and scene complexity. Despite comparably small category vocabularies, PASCAL VOC21 and Cityscapes favor distinct optimal values for 

Kmin and μ due to di ff erences in scene composition, as well as the number and scale of semantic regions within each image. As illustrated in Fig. 5, PTC achieves its best performance of 67.2% mIoU on PASCAL VOC21 with Kmin = 100 and μ = 0.3. Images in this dataset generally contain few yet salient foreground objects whose regions are relatively complete and occupy large areas, which can provide a greater amount of reliable visual evidence. Accordingly, a larger 

Kmin enables more stable visual prototypes to be constructed for well-supported categories while excluding categories supported only by sparse, scattered evidence. By contrast, PTC achieves its best performance of 40.0% mIoU on Cityscapes with Kmin = 2 and μ = 0.35. The Cityscapes dataset consists of urban street scenes that typically contain multiple semantic categories with substantial scale di ff erences, some of which are small or elongated. In this situation, an excessively large Kmin prevents categories represented by small or elongated regions from satisfying the minimum evidence requirement, reducing the number of categories eligible for calibration and weakening the gains from PTC. Notably, both PASCAL VOC21 and Cityscapes achieve better performance with relatively large values of the global calibration strength μ. This indicates that, when reliable visual prototypes can be constructed for eligible categories, appropriately increasing their influence on the text embeddings can improve visual-text matching more e ff ectively. Moreover, ADE20K and COCO-Object exhibit distinct preferences for Kmin and μ, despite comparably large class vocabularies and complex scene content. For ADE20K, the dataset covers diverse indoor and outdoor scenes, with each image typically containing multiple semantic regions. PTC achieves its best performance with Kmin = 10 and μ = 0.15 on this dataset, as shown in Fig. 5. This is because a moderate evidence threshold excludes insu ffi ciently supported categories without overly restricting category coverage, while a relatively conservative calibration strength mitigates interference from unreliable visual prototypes in complex scenes. Compared to the ADE20K dataset, COCO-Object images typically contain fewer categories but often include multiple object instances at di ff erent scales, together with complex scene context. 4.3.3. Ablation on Di ff erent Foundation Backbones 

Eff ect of Visual Foundation Models . To evaluate the applicability of PTC, we follow ProxyCLIP [ 12 ] by replacing its underlying vision foundation model with SAM [ 26 ], DINO [ 27 ], and DINOv2 [ 33 ], yielding distinct visual features for evaluation. As shown in Table 5, incorporating PTC consistently improves segmentation performance across all three VFM variants, demonstrating its robustness to diverse visual representations. Specifically, PTC achieves the most notable improvements on PASCAL VOC21, with gains of 2.6%, 5.9%, and 3.8% under the three VFM settings, respectively. This is mainly because PASCAL VOC21 contains fewer categories, and foreground objects in its images are generally larger and more salient, making reliable visual evidence easier to extract. By contrast, ADE20K, COCO-Stu ff , and PASCAL Context60 contain more categories and more complex scene content. Category confusion and background interference increase the uncertainty involved in visual prototype construction, resulting in more modest performance gains. Nevertheless, PTC still yields gains overall on these challenging datasets, demonstrating its eff ectiveness in improving the accuracy of visual-text alignment. These results collectively validate the generalization of PTC with di ff erent vision foundation models. 

Eff ect of CLIP Backbones . To verify the applicability of PTC, we further adopt di ff erent CLIP backbones on the DINO-B /8-based ProxyCLIP baseline. As shown in Table 6, PTC produces overall performance gains with ViT-B/16, ViT-L /14, and ViT-H /14, demonstrating that the proposed text calibration strategy can be e ff ectively applied across di ff erent CLIP backbones. Across the three CLIP backbones, PTC continues to produce the most significant improvements on PASCAL VOC21, increasing mIoU by 5.9%, 4.7%, and 2.7%, respectively. This may be because 13 the primary objects in this dataset are typically well defined, making reliable category-specific visual prototypes easier to construct. As the backbone size increases, the average gain from PTC gradually narrows, which may be because stronger CLIP representations leave less room for further improvement. Nevertheless, PTC achieves overall performance improvements on all three backbones. These findings indicate that the semantic gap between generic category text embeddings and category-specific visual representations in the current image does not entirely disappear as the representations become stronger, and that PTC remains e ff ective in mitigating this gap. Notably, when ViT-L /14 is used as the backbone, PTC produces a slight decrease on COCO-Object. This result indicates that in scenes with a large target vocabulary and strong background interference, the reliability of category-specific visual prototypes is more susceptible to these factors, thereby weakening the gains from text calibration. Despite this, the overall results demonstrate that PTC remains broadly applicable across di ff erent CLIP backbones. 

5. Conclusion 

In this work, we investigate an underexplored limitation in training-free OVSS: the semantic gap between generic category text embeddings and instance-specific visual representations, which hinders segmentation performance. To address this issue, we propose PTC, which extracts reliable visual evidence to construct category-specific prototypes and subsequently uses these prototypes to adaptively calibrate the corresponding text embeddings. In this way, the calibrated text embeddings incorporate visual cues of the corresponding category instances in the input while preserving generic category semantics, thereby improving the accuracy of visual-text alignment. PTC does not alter the feature extraction process of existing methods and requires neither additional training nor external models, thereby allowing it to serve as a plug-and-play module for various training-free OVSS methods. Experiments across eight benchmarks show that PTC significantly improves segmentation performance, confirming that establishing a perceptual link between generic category text embeddings and instance-specific visual appearance e ff ectively alleviates their semantic gap. Nevertheless, the performance of PTC remains dependent on the quality of the constructed visual prototypes. Future work will investigate more reliable prototype construction in complex scenes and further improve the robustness of text calibration.
