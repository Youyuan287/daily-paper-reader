Abstract 

Streaming 3D reconstruction requires more than a sequence of geometric predictions: it requires a persistent scene state that can incorporate new evidence and remain renderable as observations arrive. Latent spatial to-kens offer a promising representation for this purpose, but constructing them from an image collection leaves open how to maintain them online, where each observation may both revisit known regions and reveal new content. We introduce S2Tok, a feed-forward framework that maintains a size-adaptive, persistent scene state from uncalibrated image streams. Its central idea is to distinguish updates to the existing representation from selective expansion. A spatially informed transformer integrates each incoming observation with the persistent scene tokens, while a learned admission module selectively expands the representation to limit redundant stor-age. A hierarchical decoder and Gaussian head convert the evolving state into non-pixel-aligned 3D Gaussians, enabling novel-view rendering without caching previous frames. Experiments across four benchmarks demon-strate competitive streaming rendering quality with compact Gaussian representations. These results support latent spatial tokens as a persistent computational state for online 3D reconstruction, combining learned scene updates with explicit Gaussian rendering.

1 Introduction 

Streaming 3D reconstruction aims to recover a scene as images arrive, rather than after a complete video sequence. For interactive scene capture and embodied mapping, a reconstruction is useful be-fore observation is complete: it can help assess scene coverage and inform the next viewpoint or action. Each new observation may reveal unseen structure, resolve earlier ambiguities, or provide better evidence about previously observed regions. A useful reconstruction system must therefore maintain a representation of the accumulated scene that can be revised online and rendered through-out the process. This makes it a problem of maintaining scene state, not merely producing succes-sive geometric predictions. Latent spatial tokens are appealing because they provide a shared feature space for integrating evidence across views, separating the learned scene memory from the explicit geometry used for rendering. We investigate how to maintain and update such a tokenized scene state online while keeping it decodable into an explicit 3D scene. Existing work offers two complementary capabilities. Token-based methods such as ZipSplat [20], TokenGS [17], and GlobalSplat [7] decode latent scene tokens into non-pixel-aligned Gaussians, but construct these tokens by jointly processing a provided set of images. Recurrent methods such as CUT3R [22] and Point3R [24] instead update persistent memory as images arrive. Their standard streaming pipelines, however, use this memory to predict pointmaps for each view and form the scene by accumulating those predictions. We seek to connect these capabilities through a size-adaptive scene-token state that is updated online and jointly decoded into the accumulated Gaussian scene at each step. This places recurrent scene maintenance and explicit scene rendering within a shared representation. Maintaining an adaptive scene state requires separating state updates from expansion. A new view of an already observed surface may provide additional evidence about its geometry or appearance without introducing a new region, whereas a change in viewpoint may reveal previously occluded content. The former can be handled by updating existing tokens; the latter may require additional representational capacity. Retaining all incoming tokens conflates these roles and accumulates re-dundant information across overlapping views, while restricting the state to a fixed token set can create a capacity bottleneck as more of the scene is observed. Moreover, without known camera parameters, the model must infer how incoming observations correspond to the accumulated scene. The desired representation should therefore support both spatially informed updates to existing to-kens and selective admission of additional tokens, rather than equating every new observation with an expansion of scene memory. We introduce S2Tok , a feed-forward framework for streaming 3D Gaussian reconstruction of static scenes from uncalibrated image streams. S2Tok maintains an adaptive set of latent spatial tokens as the persistent scene state and separates updates to existing tokens from selective expansion. A Stream-Spatial Transformer integrates incoming frame tokens into the existing spatial token space and updates the spatial tokens according to the new frame. A learned admission module then selects the frame tokens predicted to carry novel content. A hierarchical decoder and Gaussian head map the resulting tokens to non-pixel-aligned Gaussians in a shared reference frame. Because the accu-mulated scene is decoded anew at each step, incoming evidence can update the representation of previously observed regions rather than only contribute additional framewise outputs. The updated spatial tokens are carried forward without caching previous frames, supporting continued reconstruc-tion and novel-view rendering. A geometric novelty criterion provides useful guidance for token admission, but does not directly measure how retaining a token affects the reconstructed scene. Because admission decisions are dis-crete, rendering losses cannot directly train the policy through ordinary backpropagation. We there-fore bootstrap the admission module by distilling a depth-based geometric novelty oracle, then refine it using self-critical sequence training. This refinement compares the reconstruction quality under

The policy can thus adjust its admission decisions according to their effect on reconstruction, rather than remaining solely tied to geometric novelty labels. Our contributions are threefold: 

• Persistent scene-state representation. We introduce a feed-forward streaming framework that maintains a size-adaptive set of latent spatial tokens and jointly decodes the accumulated Gaussian scene from the current state, without retaining past images at inference time.

The policy is initialized with geometric supervision and subsequently optimized using reconstruction-driven policy gradients. 

• Compact streaming reconstruction. Across four benchmarks, we evaluate both intermediate and final reconstructions, demonstrating competitive rendering performance with compact Gaussian representations. 

2 Related Work 

Offline 3D Reconstruction. Recent advances in 3D reconstruction cover a broad range of static and dynamic settings [8, 9, 21, 23, 28, 30, 31]. Among feed-forward approaches, DUSt3R [23] formulates uncalibrated reconstruction as pairwise pointmap regression, with global alignment for larger image collections, while VGGT [21] jointly predicts camera parameters and dense geometry from multiple views. For renderable scene reconstruction, pixelSplat [2], MVSplat [3], and DepthSplat [26] pre-dict pixel-aligned Gaussian primitives from posed images. NoPoSplat [27] removes the requirement for input camera poses by predicting Gaussians directly in a shared canonical coordinate system. UniQueR [15] moves away from pixel-aligned Gaussian prediction by using 3D queries that decode into Gaussians. These methods jointly process images without a persistent scene representation that evolves with an incoming stream. Most of those relying on pixel-aligned predictions also incur higher storage costs. 

Streaming 3D Reconstruction. Optimization-based monocular Gaussian SLAM methods [14] contin-uously estimate camera poses and update explicit Gaussian maps from RGB streams, but rely on itera-tive test-time tracking and map optimization. Recent feed-forward methods instead integrate observa-tions through causal attention or recurrent state updates. StreamVGGT [34] caches historical keys and values for causal geometric inference, while IncVGGT [5] compresses and prunes historical represen-tations to bound memory consumption. Recurrent alternatives, including CUT3R [22], Point3R [24], and Point4Cast [13], integrate observations into persistent latent or spatially anchored states and pro-duce dense geometric predictions. For renderable streaming reconstruction, StreamGS [10] predicts and aggregates per-frame Gaussians, using adjacent-frame correspondences for feature refinement and redundant Gaussian merging. Flash-Mono [32] recurrently aggregates visual features to predict camera poses and Gaussian surfels for monocular SLAM, while StreamSplat [25] performs feed-forward Gaussian prediction and adaptive fusion over uncalibrated video streams. Our focus is on maintaining a size-adaptive latent scene state that is updated with incoming observations and directly decoded into non-pixel-aligned Gaussians, without retaining a historical KV cache. 

Latent Spatial Tokens for 3D Reconstruction. Latent spatial representations offer an alternative to ty-ing scene primitives to input pixels. In object-centric reconstruction, LRM [6] uses a transformer to lift image features into structured triplane features for radiance-field reconstruction. More directly re-lated to Gaussian scene reconstruction, TokenGS [17] introduces learnable scene tokens and directly regresses 3DGS coordinates, decoupling primitive count from input resolution and the number of

3 Method 

We process a stream of images sequentially. At step 𝑖 , the incoming image I𝑖 ∈ ℝ 𝐻 ×𝑊 ×3 is used to update the latent scene state and produce the current accumulated Gaussian reconstruction. As shown in Fig. 2, we first encode I𝑖 into frame tokens f𝑖 1. Secondly, we feed the existing latent spatial tokens 

S𝑖−1 from the last step and the newly encoded f𝑖 into the proposed Stream-Spatial (SS) Transformer. In the SS-Transformer, S𝑖−1 is adapted to the new frames and viewpoints; meanwhile, f𝑖 is implicitly aligned to the latent spatial space. We then apply the admission module to the updated f′𝑖 and S′𝑖−1 ,and append the selected frame tokens to form S𝑖 ∈ ℝ (𝑀+𝛿)×𝐷 , where 𝛿 is the number of admitted frame tokens. We then provide S𝑖 and token sets from two other layers of the SS-Transformer as inputs to the Hierarchical Decoder and predict Gaussian candidates with the Gaussian Head. Each token corresponds to ℎ Gaussians. Finally, S𝑖 is carried over to the next step.

3.3 Learned Admission for Selective State Expansion 

Learnable Admission Module. To control the growth of the scene-token state, we introduce a learn-able admission module consisting of a cross-attention layer and a self-attention layer. At each step, it takes f′𝑖 as query, S′𝑖−1 as key/value, and predicts admission logits 𝜋 . Frame tokens whose admission probabilities exceed 𝜏 are appended to the updated scene tokens: 

S𝑖 = S′𝑖−1 ⊕ f′𝑖 [𝜎(𝜋) > 𝜏 ], 𝜋 = Admission (𝑞𝑢𝑒𝑟𝑦 = f′𝑖 , 𝑘𝑒𝑦/𝑣𝑎𝑙𝑢𝑒 = S′𝑖−1 ) (4) Here, 𝜎 is the Sigmoid function. The resulting S𝑖 is carried to step 𝑖 + 1 , allowing f𝑖+1 to interact with the accumulated scene representation without storing a historical KV cache. 

Two-stage Learning Strategy. At the start of training, directly using the admission module’s outputs for hard token selection prevents ordinary backpropagation through the admission decisions to the admission logits, potentially reinforcing arbitrary early decisions. Such discrete selections prevent gradients from reaching the admission logits, which can push arbitrary early decisions and lead to a poor local optimum. We therefore train the admission module in two stages. First, we distill the decisions of a depth-based geometric oracle (Appendix A) using the class-weighted binary cross-entropy loss discussed in Sec. 3.5. Oracle supervision provides an initialization for admission, but does not optimize the policy accord-ing to reconstruction outcomes. We therefore further optimize the admission policy using self-critical sequence training (SCST) [18], which compares sampled and deterministic admissions through their reconstruction errors. This provides reconstruction-driven policy gradients for discrete admission de-cisions. Specifically, SCST performs two feed-forward rollouts under the same model parameters. Let p =𝜎(𝜋) denote the predicted admission probabilities. The first rollout serves as a self-critical baseline without gradient computation and applies the deterministic admission mask a𝑑 , whereas the second rollout applies a stochastic admission mask a𝑏 sampled from the Bernoulli policy: 

a𝑑 = 1 [p > 𝜏 ] , a𝑏 ∼ Bernoulli (p). (5) Both a𝑑 and a𝑏 are binary masks that can admit multiple tokens. We denote the reconstruction errors produced by the two rollouts as 𝑒 𝑑 and 𝑒 𝑏 , respectively, and define the self-critical advantage as

The admission module is then optimized using the SCST objective discussed in Sec. 3.5. 4 Experiments 

We train S2Tok solely on DL3DV [12] and RealEstate10K [33]. More training, testing, and im-plementation details can be found in Sec. D in the Appendix. For evaluations, we show extensive quantitative and qualitative comparisons between S2Tok and other baselines. We get the rendering performance of pointmap prediction methods using point-splat rendering [16, 35]. Specifically, we hold out one target view between consecutive context views and evaluate recon-struction quality in two settings: 1) Final : Render all target views using the final reconstruction after processing the whole context sequence. 2) Intermediate : After each step, render the correspond-ing target view using the current reconstruction, based only on the context views observed so far. Streaming methods update the scene incrementally, whereas non-streaming methods jointly process all available context views anew at each evaluation step. StreamSplat is evaluated only under Inter-mediate , as its time-conditioned reconstruction does not produce a globally camera-aligned final scene.

5 Conclusion 

We propose S2Tok , a feed-forward framework that maintains a size-adaptive latent scene state for 3D Gaussian reconstruction from uncalibrated image streams. By separating observation integration from selective state expansion, S2Tok incorporates incoming frames into a shared token represen-tation and jointly decodes the accumulated scene without retaining past images at inference time. Experiments across four benchmarks demonstrate competitive rendering performance with compact Gaussian outputs. These results support persistent spatial tokens as a practical representation for streaming scene reconstruction. Improving fidelity retention over continued updates and controlling state growth over longer streams remain important directions for future work.

A Admission Pseudo-GT 

We generate pseudo-GT admission labels from per-frame depth maps, camera intrinsics, and camera poses that are available only during training; at test time the admission module operates on images alone. The pseudo-GT criterion is a depth-reprojection novelty test that marks the image patches observing geometrically unseen regions of the scene. 

Pseudo-GT depth and cameras. Camera intrinsics 𝐾 𝑖 and camera-to-reference poses 𝑇 𝑖 are taken directly from the dataset annotations (COLMAP or capture metadata); all poses are expressed rela-tive to the first frame of the clip. Only the depth maps are predicted. We run DA3 [11] (the nested Giant-Large checkpoint) offline over every training clip, conditioning it on the images together with the dataset intrinsics and world-to-camera extrinsics, and requesting depth aligned to the scale of the input extrinsics. The resulting depth maps therefore live in the same metric gauge as the pose translations, which makes reprojected depths directly comparable across frames and keeps the scale-normalization targets below consistent with the geometry supervision. Clips whose length exceeds the DA3 context budget of 150 views are processed in round-robin interleaved chunks (frame 𝑖 is assigned to chunk 𝑖 mod 𝑛 𝑐 ), so each forward pass spans the full trajectory and the extrinsic-scale alignment stays globally consistent across chunks. Depths are stored with log-space 8-bit quantiza-tion whose range is set by the 0.1 /99.9 depth percentiles ( < 1% relative error); out-of-range pixels are marked invalid.

Causality of the labels. Although DA3 [11] processes each training clip as a whole when producing the teacher depth maps, the coverage comparison is restricted to the observed prefix: the label for frame 𝑖 compares frame 𝑖 only against the observed prefix {0, … , 𝑖 − 1} , never against future frames. Prefix scale for normalization. The same precomputation also yields a per-step scene scale used to normalize pose-translation and depth supervision. D Implementation Details 

D.1 Architecture Details 

We use the weights of the pre-trained ZipSplat ViT-G/14 with a patch size of 𝑤 = 14 to initialize our model. Notably, ZipSplat is trained on the same datasets as S2Tok. We initialize the local scene attention with the same weights as the local frame attention. Our encoder contains 13 self-attention Transformer blocks to process only the incoming frame with a feature dimension of 𝐷 = 1536 . Our SS-Transformer contains 27 blocks, each composed of a global attention layer, a local scene attention layer, and a local frame attention layer. Features from the 7th, 17th, and 27th blocks in the SS-Transformer are passed to the hierarchical decoder. Each spatial token is decoded into ℎ = 32 Gaussians with degree-1 spherical harmonics. The admission module first projects the frame and spatial tokens to 256 dimensions, followed by one cross-attention layer, one self-attention layer, and an MLP classifier. At inference, a frame token is admitted when 𝜎(𝜋) > 𝜏 , where 𝜏 = 0.5 .

D.2 Color-Skip Branch 

We additionally use a 128-dimensional color-skip branch adapted to our streaming setting from the color-skip branch in ZipSplat. Following ZipSplat [20], we attach a 128 -dimensional color-skip branch that routes high-frequency appearance around the backbone directly to the Gaussian head. In ZipSplat this branch embeds all 

context views at once and lets the retained tokens cross-attend to the color tokens of every view; this is incompatible with streaming, where past frames are discarded. We therefore restructure the branch around a per-token color bank that is maintained in lockstep with the scene memory. When frame 𝑖 arrives, a lightweight patch embedding maps it to per-patch color tokens c𝑖 ∈ ℝ 𝑇 ×128 , and only the newly admitted patches query a single cross-attention block (operating entirely in the 128 -dimensional color space) whose keys and values are the color tokens of the current frame only .The resulting color features are appended to the color bank, row-aligned with the newly admitted scene-memory tokens. The two memories are updated asymmetrically. Scene-memory tokens are re-contextualized by the backbone at every step, so the geometric feature of an old token contin-ues to evolve as new observations arrive. Color-bank entries, in contrast, are write-once : the color feature of a token is computed exactly once, from the frame that admitted it, and is retained frozen thereafter—it is never recomputed, never passes through the backbone or decoder, and receives no re-current update. At every step, the Gaussian head consumes the concatenation of each token’s current 

> 18 S2Tok: Streaming 3D Gaussian Reconstruction

decoded feature with its frozen color feature, for old and newly admitted tokens alike. The branch computes new color features from the current frame and reuses previously stored color-bank entries without revisiting past images. It supplies admission-time appearance features to the Gaussian head alongside the recurrently updated scene features. 

D.3 Training Details 

Training consists of three stages. In the first stage, we train the model together with our proposed ad-mission module, but use the depth-reprojection novelty oracle (Appendix A) to select from new frame tokens. This provides a stable admission policy for training the other modules, including the Encoder, SS-Transformer, Hierarchical Decoder, and Gaussian Head. The admission module is trained concurrently by distilling the oracle decisions, but its outputs are not used for frame-token selection in this stage. We train for 100 epochs in the first stage on 128 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs, with the overall training loss: 

ℒ = ℒrender + ℒchamfer + ℒscale + ℒbce (26) In the second stage, we enable SCST training and use the outputs of the admission module itself to select frame tokens. This stage lets the admission module learn directly from reconstruction perfor-mance, and lets the other modules dynamically adapt to the newly learned policy. We train for 100 epochs in the second stage on 128 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs, with the overall training loss: 

ℒ = ℒrender + ℒchamfer + ℒscale + ℒscst (27) In the first two stages, we train S2Tok using context sequences whose lengths are uniformly sampled from [12, 16] .In the final stage, we freeze the parameters of the admission module and train all remaining compo-nents of S2Tok for 74 epochs on 64 NVIDIA B200 GPUs. This stage adapts the remaining trainable components to deterministic admission while holding the admission-module parameters fixed. To be precise, only the admission-module parameters are frozen: the encoder and SS-Transformer features supplied to the module continue to be trained, so the admission probabilities are recomputed online at every step from the current features rather than replayed from precomputed masks. In this stage, ad-mission is no longer Bernoulli-sampled; each patch is admitted deterministically when its admission probability exceeds 0.5 , which is exactly the decision rule used at inference. The module inputs are detached, so no gradient flows from the admission decision into the backbone. Admission decisions can still change as the backbone features evolve. This stage therefore trains the remaining compo-nents under the deterministic admission rule used at inference, rather than the Bernoulli-sampled rule used in stage two. D.4 Hyperparameters 

We set 𝜆 lpips = 0.05 , 𝜆 depth = 0.01 , 𝜆 solo render = 0.5 , 𝜆 chamfer = 0.05 , 𝜆 solo chamfer = 0.05 , and 𝜆 scale = 1 .For the depth-reprojection novelty teacher, we use a relative depth tolerance of 𝜖 geo = 0.08 and admit a patch when its novel-pixel fraction exceeds 0.3 . Because the scene subsets differ between the 12-view and 50-view evaluations for some datasets, comparisons across these settings should not be interpreted as a controlled sequence-length ablation. 

D.5 Evaluation Protocol 

For the 12-view setting, we evaluate on 8 DL3DV, 8 RealEstate10K, 9 Mip-NeRF 360, and 8 ScanNet++ scenes. For the 50-view setting, we evaluate on all held-out evaluation sets of DL3DV, RealEstate10K, and ScanNet++, and all scenes of Mip-NeRF 360. Context views are sampled in temporal order, with one target view between each pair of consecutive context views. In Intermedi-ate evaluation, target 𝑡 𝑘−1 is rendered after observing context views 𝑐 0, … , 𝑐 𝑘 ; in Final evaluation, all targets are rendered after observing the complete context stream. 

E Datasets 

Training datasets. We train our model on only two datasets: RealEstate10K [33] and DL3DV [12]. Specifically, we use their training sets and keep the held-out evaluation sets for testing.

Preprocessing and streaming sampling. All images are resized with the shorter side set to 252 pixels, and their spatial dimensions are made divisible by the ViT patch size. G More Results 

We put more results in this section to more concretely demonstrate the effectiveness of S2Tok. Tab. 5, Tab. 6, and Tab. 7 provide detailed results on RealEstate10K, Mip-NeRF 360, and Scan-Net++. S2Tok combines compact Gaussian outputs with strong PSNR results among the compared feed-forward streaming methods, but does not lead every metric or setting. In particular, Stream-Splat achieves better results in the 50-view Intermediate evaluation on ScanNet++, and several methods obtain lower LPIPS in other comparisons. MonoGS obtains higher 50-view Final PSNR on RealEstate10K and ScanNet++, while using per-scene optimization and requiring substantially more reconstruction time in the timing experiment of Tab. 1. Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10, and Fig. 6 illustrate qualitative differences between the evaluated reconstruc-tions. In selected examples, S2Tok produces fewer isolated artifacts than several compared render-ings, although blur and geometry errors remain. Architecturally, S2Tok predicts the current Gaussian scene jointly from its updated token representation in a shared reference frame, rather than treating previously decoded Gaussians as immutable outputs. This enables scene-level readout from the car-ried state, but does not by itself guarantee geometric accuracy or fidelity retention across updates. The comparisons also involve differences in supervision and rendering representations, so they do not isolate a single cause of the observed performance gaps. Fig. 11 reports the storage of the method-specific representation components identified in the legend, averaged over the evaluated 50-view sequences. The growth of S2Tok token storage becomes slower within this evaluation window, but the state continues to expand. This finite-horizon observation does not establish a bound on memory usage for longer streams. Moreover, these component-level storage measurements are distinct from end-to-end GPU memory consumption, for which Tab. 1 reports peak usage. 

H Limitations 

S2Tok maintains a persistent scene state, but continued updates can reduce rendering fidelity on previously evaluated views, as reflected by the Final–Intermediate gaps in several settings. The model is trained on sequences of 12–24 context frames and evaluated quantitatively at 12 and 50 views; robust retention over longer streams remains unestablished. Admission controls which new tokens are added, but the current model does not remove or compress admitted tokens. State size and the
