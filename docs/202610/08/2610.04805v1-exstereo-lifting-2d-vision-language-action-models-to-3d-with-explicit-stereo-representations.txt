# ExStereo: Lifting 2D Vision-Language-Action Models to 3D with Explicit Stereo Representations 

I-Chun Arthur Liu * Jason Chen * Gaurav S. Sukhatme Daniel Seita Viterbi School of Engineering, University of Southern California 

Abstract —Three-dimensional perception is critical for robotic manipulation, particularly for high-precision tasks, as recovering metric depth and precise 3D object positions from monocular RGB observations is inherently ill-posed. However, many Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models rely solely on RGB observations for perception. Leveraging recent advances in foundation models for stereo matching, we introduce ExStereo, a stereo module that augments pre-trained 2D VLAs with 3D perception. ExStereo reconstructs scene geometry from stereo image pairs and renders multi-view observations as an explicit stereo representation for stereo feature extraction. The action tokens from the action expert selectively attend to the resulting stereo tokens through our proposed action-stereo cross-attention mechanism, enabling the policy to generate robot actions conditioned on 3D scene information. To learn robust 3D representations, we introduce a mid-training stage before task-specific post-training, using a self-supervised learning objective on large-scale stereo data. We validate our approach by fine-tuning two publicly available VLAs, π0.5 and SmolVLA, and evaluate them in simulation and on a real-world bimanual PiPER platform. Across both settings, VLAs fine-tuned with ExStereo consistently outperform baselines, demonstrating the effectiveness of stereo perception for robotic manipulation. Our anonymous project website is available at https://exstereo-vla.github.io/ExStereo/. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have recently emerged as a powerful paradigm for robotic manipulation [1]– [3], demonstrating impressive visuomotor capabilities. By building on large-scale pretrained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) [4, 5], these policies learn from semantic priors, acquire diverse skills from demonstrations, and generalize across objects and instructions. However, most VLA models operate on monocular RGB observations processed through pre-trained 2D vision encoders to extract features for down-stream action generation. Operating on 2D visual tokens, these policies lack precise depth and geometric information. While effective in controlled settings, real-world interactions often require accurate spatial relationships and object locations, as well as robustness to occlusions and visual variations. Recent work has explored injecting 3D structure into VLAs [6]–[9], achieving strong performance on 3D manip-ulation tasks. However, these methods depend on RGB-D sensors, which require precise calibration and are sensitive to sensor noise. Meanwhile, StereoVLA [10] and StereoPol-icy [11] leverage stereo image pairs to obtain implicit stereo features for policy learning. A. Vision-Language-Action Models 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, also commonly referred to as robotic foundation models, fine-tune pretrained VLMs to output robot actions, such as end-effector pose deltas or joint targets, by training on large-scale teleoperation data [14]. Representative systems include RT-2, OpenVLA [1], SmolVLA [15], and the π family [2, 16]–[18]. We refer the reader to [19, 20] for surveys. Early VLAs discretized actions into tokens and predicted them autoregressively [1, 21], whereas recent policies attach a dedicated action expert trained with diffusion or flow matching to produce smooth, high-frequency action chunks [2, 15, 16]. Camera configurations have broadened in parallel, from a single third-person view to wrist-mounted cameras and to multi-camera rigs that observe the workspace from several sides [2]. Across these variants, each view is still processed by a monocular 2D encoder so metric scale and inter-object spatial relations must be inferred from appearance. Prior work has addressed these limitations by incorporating depth sensors [22]–[24], such as RGB-D cameras. However, these sensors often degrade on transparent [24] and reflective sur-faces and are sensitive to sensor noise, calibration errors, and point cloud artifacts, making high-quality 3D reconstruc-tion challenging. Beyond these hardware limitations, visual representations remain a key bottleneck. Existing 2D vision backbones [4, 25, 26] generalize well across diverse visual domains but lack explicit 3D structure. In contrast, 3D vision backbones directly capture geometry from point clouds [27]– [30], but tend to underperform their 2D counterparts due to the scarcity of large-scale 3D training data. These limitations motivate a 3D perception approach that preserves the scalability and representational strengths of 2D vision while providing explicit geometric information. Stereo setups are common on robot platforms and can recover such geometry from binocular disparity, yet the second image is rarely exploited by the policy. ExStereo builds on this observation and equips pre-trained VLAs with an explicit 3D representation computed from a stereo pair. 

B. Stereo Vision in Computer Vision 

Stereo matching recovers metric depth by estimating dis-parity between a rectified left–right image pair without active depth sensors. Previous classical methods build a cost vol-ume and refine disparity with 3D convolutions or recurrent updates [31]–[33]. More recently, the field has moved toward stereo foundation models that generalize zero-shot across domains by transferring priors from monocular depth models. FoundationStereo [34] establishes this direction through large-scale pretraining, and later work improves robustness on transparent, reflective, and textureless surfaces where depth sensors fail [35, 36]. Fast-FoundationStereo [12] distills these models for near real-time inference. We leverage it in ExStereo to obtain an explicit 3D representation. 

C. Stereo Vision in Robotics 

Stereo cameras recover depth from binocular disparity, making them more reliable for transparent, specular, and textureless objects, where conventional depth sensors often struggle. Prior work has applied stereo vision to challenging materials such as glass [24, 37], as well as to stereo-based grasping and tracking [38]. More recently, StereoPolicy [11] and StereoVLA [10] extract features from stereo modules, which we characterize as implicit representations (see Section IV-A for details), and combine them with language or RGB image features as inputs to a VLM for policy learning. In contrast, we use a stereo module to construct an explicit 3D representation of the scene and feed the corresponding tokens directly into the action expert for policy learning. III. ExStereo is the stereo module ψ that takes a stereo image pair as input, generates stereo tokens, and fuses them with action tokens from the VLA for 3D-conditioned action gener-ation. It first constructs a 3D scene point cloud for multi-view observation rendering (Section IV-A). A. Multi-View Observation Rendering 

For a rectified stereo pair image (ILt , IRt ), we input it into a pre-trained, frozen Fast-FoundationStereo [12] F , a foundation model for stereo depth estimation, to obtain a dense disparity map dt = F (ILt , IRt ). We use the disparity map, horizontal focal length fx from K, and B to compute metric depth Zt = fxBdt . We then back-project the depth map using K

to obtain a metric 3D point cloud in the left camera coordinate frame. To ensure consistent multi-view observations, we align the point cloud to a shared world coordinate frame: camera coordinates are remapped to a conventional world frame ( X

right, Y forward, Z up). The dominant plane, corresponding to the table surface, is detected using RANSAC [39] and rotated to be level such that its normal is aligned with the +Z axis. The table surface is then shifted to z = 0, and the point cloud is horizontally re-centered by mean-centering the X and Y

coordinates. We place four “virtual” cameras at orthogonal locations around the origin and render the point cloud into four orthographic views (Top, Front, Left, and Right) using a z-buffer that retains the nearest surface at each pixel. Each view is rasterized at a resolution of 128 × 128 and contains depth, 3D coordinates, and a validity mask. Note that the depth values are expressed in each virtual view’s coordinate frame, whereas the 3D coordinates are expressed in the world frame. The four views are concatenated horizontally to form the multi-view observation Ot ∈ R128 ×512 ×5.We define the explicit reconstruction of the point cloud and its multi-view rendering as an explicit stereo representation, in contrast to implicit stereo representations, which directly process stereo image pairs through a stereo module without explicitly reconstructing the underlying 3D geometry. This explicit representation enables the policy to perceive and reason about the environment in metric 3D space.

C. Mid-Training and Post-Training 

For VLA fine-tuning, we train πθ together with ψ in two stages: mid-training and post-training. Mid-training is an intermediate stage between pre-training a VLA on broad data and post-training on task-specific demonstrations. As shown empirically in our experiments, mid-training is particularly important for methods that introduce a new, unseen module after pre-training, as it allows the module to gradually adapt during policy learning. In this work, we mid-train πθ together with ψ on a large-scale stereo dataset to learn generalizable 3D representations for bimanual manipulation tasks. During mid-training, we leverage StereoEngine [40] to gen-erate a large-scale stereo dataset for real-world experiments. StereoEngine is a recent method that uses video diffusion to either lift existing monocular demonstrations into stereo pairs or directly generate both stereo views from simulation. In addition to the original VLA training objective (e.g., the flow-matching action loss), we introduce a masked autoencoding (MAE) [41] objective on the multi-view observations Ot .Specifically, at each training step, we uniformly sample and mask a subset of the 16 × 16 patches in Ot , producing a masked multi-view observation ˜Ot . C. Simulation Results 

Table I reports the test success rates of different methods in simulation. VLAs fine-tuned with ExStereo (columns high-lighted in green) demonstrate significant performance gains, with improvements of up to 50.6% for π0.5 and 65.3% for SmolVLA on Handover Mic . On average, ExStereo im-proves the success rates of π0.5 and SmolVLA by 25.2% and 23.3%, respectively. Moreover, VLAs with ExStereo outper-form the other baselines across all tasks. Qualitatively, VLAs fine-tuned with ExStereo are more precise when grasping and manipulating objects, including reorienting, handing over, and pressing them. With respect to π0.5-ExStereo (w/o MT), it outperforms the post-trained base π0.5 on all tasks. This demonstrates that post-training alone with ExStereo can consistently improve performance over the base VLA across a diverse set of tasks. With the inclusion of the mid-training stage, performance further improves on the majority of tasks. We attribute the performance degradation with mid-training on certain tasks to the choice of tasks used during mid-training. Specifically, we hypothesize that including mid-training tasks that are more similar to the post-training tasks in terms of task type and environmental variations may help address this issue.

B. Datasets 

To perform mid-training, ExStereo requires large-scale real-world stereo bimanual datasets. Existing datasets, such as DROID [46] and RoboCOIN [47], are predominantly either single-arm or monocular. In addition, few AgileX PiPER datasets are publicly available, and those that do exist are pre-dominantly monocular. We therefore collect as many publicly available AgileX PiPER datasets from the Internet as we can find, yielding 20 datasets from 11 contributors and a total of 3469 clips. We also include datasets collected using the AgileX Cobot Magic robot, which extends the AgileX PiPER with a mobile base. We provide a complete list of the datasets used in the Appendix. After collecting these datasets, we feed the monocular left views into StereoEngine [40] to generate a large-scale stereo dataset consisting of left-right image pairs while preserving the action labels from the original data. We then use the resulting dataset for policy mid-training. For post-training, an experienced roboticist used a Meta Quest 2 to collect teleoperation data. Because each task has its own difficulty, we collect a different number of demonstrations for each task: 200 for Stack Two Bowls , 50 for Lift Glass Bottles , and 20 for Close Fridge Door .

C. Real-World Results 

Real-world results are presented in Table III, with exam-ple rollouts of π0.5-ExStereo shown in Figure 4. ExStereo demonstrates substantially improved robustness across all tasks compared to baselines. Aggregated over the three tasks, 

π0.5-ExStereo succeeds on 50/60 trials (83.3%) versus 16/60 (26.7%) for the π0.5 baseline, with π0.5-ExStereo w/o MT in between at 36/60 (60.0%). StereoVLA, which uses implicit stereo features, performs worst at 11/60 (18.3%), falling below the RGB-only π0.5 baseline on every task. We hypothesize that StereoVLA has weaker generalization capabilities than π0.5, which is pre-trained on a larger and more diverse dataset. Combined with its VLM-based approach using implicit stereo features, post-training on a limited number of task demonstrations may be insufficient for StereoVLA to reach its optimal performance. The largest gain is on Lift Glass Bottles , where ExStereo reaches 18/20 while the 

π0.5 baseline reaches only 5/20 and StereoVLA only 4/20. 

Stack Two Bowls: The baseline methods frequently failed to grasp the bowls, either closing the gripper beside or above them. π0.5-ExStereo w/o MT and StereoVLA reliably grasped the left bowl but often failed to grasp the right bowl and stack it on top. In contrast, the RGB-only π0.5 baselines failed to grasp either bowl, highlighting the importance of 3D perception for successfully grasping these objects. 

Lift Glass Bottles: The most common failure modes for the baselines were failing to pick up the bottles and knocking them over with the gripper. In contrast, π0.5-ExStereo is the most reliable policy for precisely grasping and lifting bottles. VII. L IMITATIONS 

First, ExStereo depends on a pre-trained stereo foundation model [12], which may degrade on distant, textureless, and thin structures, and these errors may affect the reconstructed multi-view observations. Second, the projected 3D point cloud is derived from a single stereo viewpoint, so occluded regions leave holes across all four rendered views. Third, our multi-view rendering assumes a planar surface, such as a tabletop, to define the world frame, which may not hold in cluttered or non-tabletop scenes. Finally, our mid-training data comes from a single benchmark and a single bimanual embodiment. Scaling to larger stereo robot datasets and more diverse embodiments remains an important direction for future work. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

We present ExStereo, a stereo module that equips any pre-trained 2D VLA with 3D perception using only a stereo camera by reconstructing explicit scene geometry from astereo pair with an off-the-shelf stereo foundation model, rendering the geometry into a set of multi-view observations, and injecting the resulting stereo tokens into the base policy through our proposed action-stereo cross-attention mechanism. We mid-train the VLA with ExStereo on large-scale stereo data using a self-supervised objective and further post-train the model on a small set of task demonstrations while keeping ExStereo frozen. Across simulation and real-world experiments, VLAs fine-tuned with ExStereo substantially outperform their base policies, as well as RGB-, point-cloud-, and stereo-based baselines. Furthermore, we demonstrate that explicitly representing stereo geometry and leveraging it in the action expert is more effective than the standard VLM approach with implicit stereo geometry for direct fine-tuning. These results demonstrate that incorporating stereo vision is a promising approach for augmenting existing 2D VLAs with 3D spatial perception. IX. A CKNOWLEDGMENT

A. Implementation Details 

ExStereo. The ExStereo module adds 21 .2M parameters to a VLA (3 .2M for the multi-view ViT, 17 .3M for the action-stereo cross-attention projections, and 0 .6M for the MAE decoder, which is discarded after mid-training). This is a minimal addition: for reference, π0.5 has a 2B-parameter VLM backbone and a 300M-parameter action expert. We mid-train the VLA with ExStereo for 300 , 000 training steps, followed by 30 , 000 steps of post-training. For π0.5 and SmolVLA, we use their default hyper-parameters: those from RoboTwin for 

π0.5 and from the LeRobot GitHub repository for SmolVLA. We do not perform any hyper-parameter tuning and directly use the checkpoint from the final training step for evaluation. See Table IV for the hyper-parameters used in the ExStereo module.

As with ExStereo, we do not perform any hyperparameter tuning and use the default hyper-parameters from their respective codebases: the official StereoVLA GitHub repository for StereoVLA and RoboTwin for ACT and DP3. StereoVLA is trained for 30 , 000 steps, while ACT and DP3 are trained for 6 , 000 and 3 , 000 epochs, respectively.

B. Domain Randomization 

We use the default domain randomization settings provided in the RoboTwin documentation. Specifically, we enable ran-dom backgrounds by applying random textures to the table and background, and add table clutter using distractor objects to simulate cluttered environments. The clean-background rate is set to 0 .02, such that 2% of samples retain a clean background despite background randomization.
