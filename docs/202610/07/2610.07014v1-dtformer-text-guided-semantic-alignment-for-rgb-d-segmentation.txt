# DTFormer: Text-Guided Semantic Alignment for RGB-D Segmentation 

Ziang Wei 2, Yinlong Liu 3, Yan Xia 4, Alois Knoll 2, and Hu Cao 1∗

Abstract —RGB-D semantic segmentation has made notable progress by fusing RGB and Depth, yet mainstream models still learn features almost exclusively from pixel-level supervision, lacking direct high-level semantic constraints. This raises acentral question: can external knowledge—such as language pri-ors—inject stronger semantic discriminability into mainstream RGB-D segmentation models? We present DTFormer, a novel tri-modal (RGB-D-Text) semantic segmentation framework. At its core is Text-guided Semantic Alignment Module (TSAM) that first encodes textual cues into a set of semantic prototypes and then explicitly aligns multi-modal RGB-D features with these prototypes at multiple encoder and decoder layers. This design imposes strong semantic regularization on representa-tion learning, guiding the network toward more discriminative features. Extensive experiments on multiple benchmarks show that DTFormer delivers consistent gains while remaining simple and efficient. Our results demonstrate that explicit semantic alignment offers an effective and practical route to improving RGB-D semantic segmentation. The code will be released upon acceptance. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# SEMANTIC segmentation, a core task in computer vision, assigns a semantic label to every pixel, and it underpins applications including autonomous driving [1]–[3], robotics [4], [5], augmented reality [6], and medical imaging [7]. With the advance of 3D sensing technologies, RGB-D data has emerged as a robust paradigm for scene understanding, particularly under occlusion and low-light conditions [8]– [10]. In parallel, large-scale vision-language models [11]–[13] have demonstrated that textual knowledge can provide class-level anchors and transferable high-level semantics for dense prediction, as evidenced in text-driven 2D segmentation [14]– [16]. However, a tri-modal paradigm that injects such language priors into geometrically-aware RGB-D segmentation remains surprisingly underexplored. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the evolution of semantic segmen-tation can be characterized by the modalities each pipeline leverages. Conventional RGB-only methods (Fig. 1(a)) feed a single image to an encoder-decoder [18], [20], but they remain constrained by appearance and texture cues alone, often failing under illumination changes, occlusion, or texture ambiguity. To address this, RGB-D segmentation (Fig. 1(b)) introduces depth as a geometric cue to alleviate such ambiguities; for instance, DFormerv2 [19] treats depth as a geometry prior that modulates self-attention, achieving a better accuracy-efficiency

However, existing RGB-D pipelines still rely mainly on pixel-level supervision and geometric constraints, largely overlooking the high-level semantic regularities available in textual form. Recently, the success of vision-language models (VLMs) [11], [13] has inspired textual priors for dense prediction. Early methods such as DenseCLIP [16] and LSeg [14] align visual features with fixed CLIP class-name embeddings, but applying a single dataset-level vocabulary to every image introduces semantic ambiguity from text tokens irrelevant to the actual scene. Subsequent learnable-query approaches like X-Decoder [21] and SEEM [22] achieve more flexible open-vocabulary segmentation, yet, as general-purpose models, they incur addi-tional computation and rarely model modality-specific charac-teristics. Moreover, most VLM-based segmentation pipelines inject language only at the decoder [14], [16], [21], [22], missing the opportunity to guide feature learning throughout the encoder. To address these limitations, we introduce the Text-guided Semantic Alignment Module (TSAM), which bridges RGB-D fused visual features and textual modalities through temperature-scaled cross-attention (TSCA) with stage-adaptive designs. As illustrated in Fig. 2, TSAM learns to selectively attend to semantically relevant regions based on different arXiv:2610.07014v1 [cs.CV] 4 Oct 2026 2

class names, enabling fine-grained semantic-visual alignment. Building on TSAM, we construct DTFormer, a hierarchical text-guided framework for RGB-D segmentation that leverages vision-language models to generate image-specific text priors (Fig. 3) and injects them at multiple stages throughout the network. Unlike methods that rely on fixed class names or inject language only at the decoder, DTFormer employs a dual-variant strategy: a lightweight Text-guided Semantic Alignment for Encoder (TSA-E) enables semantic feature extraction with minimal overhead, while an enriched Text-guided Semantic Alignment for Decoder (TSA-D) provides fine-grained semantic-geometric alignment with additional normalization and an MLP. Built upon the DFormerv2 [19] backbone, DTFormer effectively fuses RGB, depth geometry, and language semantics, creating a tri-modal representation for robust scene understanding. We evaluate DTFormer on two widely-used RGB-D seman-tic segmentation benchmarks, NYU Depth V2 [23] and SUN-RGBD [24]. Our method achieves consistent improvements across all model scales (Tab. I): DTFormer-S attains 57.8% mIoU on NYU Depth V2 and 53.5% on SUN-RGBD, while DTFormer-L establishes new state-of-the-art (SOTA) results with 58.6% and 53.9% mIoU respectively, surpassing previous best methods with competitive computational efficiency. These results validate that hierarchical injection of image-specific language priors can effectively complement geometric reason-ing in RGB-D segmentation. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:  

> •

We propose the Text-guided Semantic Alignment Module (TSAM) with stage-adaptive encoder/decoder variants, injecting language priors across the network to enable hierarchical text–visual-geometry alignment.  

> •

We present DTFormer, a tri-modal RGB–Depth–Text framework that fuses VLM-generated, image-specific text priors with RGB-D representations via multi-stage align-ment, addressing the lack of high-level semantic con-straints in existing RGB-D segmentation methods.  

> •

RGB-D Semantic Segmentation. RGB-only semantic seg-mentation often suffers from significant performance degra-dation in complex real-world scenarios, such as low-light conditions or cluttered environments. The integration of depth data, which provides crucial 3D geometry information, has led to the development of RGB-D semantic segmentation [25], [26]. Early approaches [27], [28] utilized dual-stream encoders to process RGB and depth in separate backbones, followed by various feature fusion schemes. Subsequent works introduced more sophisticated fusion mechanisms, such as designing attention-based gates [8], [29] or shape-aware operations [30], [31]. With the advent of Transformers, this paradigm was extended by [9], [32], [33], which employ cross-modal interac-tion modules—e.g., cross-attention or token replacement—to exchange information between modalities. To address the high computational cost of dual-stream models, an alternative line explores efficient or more tightly coupled encoder designs [34]–[36]. Most recently, DFormerv2 [19] takes this further, treating depth not as another encoded feature but as an explicit geometry prior that directly guides the self-attention weights of the RGB extractor. While these methods mark significant advancements in fusing photometric and geometric informa-tion, they rely exclusively on visual-spatial cues, overlooking the rich contextual guidance offered by language. 

Vision-Language for Dense Prediction. Large-scale vision-language models, particularly CLIP [11], have inspired text-guided dense prediction. Early works in text-guided seg-mentation, such as DenseCLIP [16], LSeg [14], and CLIPSeg [37], align pixel-level features with text embeddings and are often instantiated with dataset-level vocabularies. While effective, this approach introduces semantic ambiguity, as the model must disambiguate against irrelevant text tokens. This limitation motivated efforts to replace fixed, dataset-level vocabularies with more flexible text inputs. One popular approach [21], [22], [38], [39] utilizes learnable text queries to segment arbitrary concepts. A parallel line of work [40]– [42] focuses on text-to-box grounding to achieve region-level segmentation. Despite their power, these methods share two fundamental limitations in the context of our research: (1) they are designed as general-purpose 2D models, overlooking modality-specific characteristics such as depth geometry, and (2) a common choice is to inject language priors primarily at the decoder stage, which may miss the opportunity to guide early-stage feature extraction. Unlike these approaches, DTFormer generates image-specific class names via VLMs and injects text priors hierarchically in both encoder and decoder stages, introducing text guidance atop geometry-aware RGB-D backbones. Exploiting scene sparsity: Indoor scenes typically contain only a small subset of the full class set. To exploit this natural sparsity, we impose a weak cardinality constraint by instructing each VLM to output at most 8 class names (mmax = 8 ). With two VLMs ( K = 2 ), the intersection L

typically yields a stable set of 4–8 class names per image, balancing precision and recall. 

VLM implementation: We employ two high-performance vision-language models from the InternVL [49] and Qwen-VL [50] families as our dual VLMs ( K = 2 ). Both models query each image with a fixed prompt template that instructs them to output up to 8 class names from the dataset vocabulary, prioritizing large and obvious regions. The prompt explicitly constrains outputs to match exact spellings from the provided vocabulary to minimize hallucination. Intersection filtering across both VLM outputs ensures only consistently predicted class names are retained, reducing model-specific biases while maintaining sufficient semantic coverage. 

VLM query prompt: To ensure reliable and vocabulary-constrained outputs, we design a structured prompt that in-structs VLMs to generate class names based on image content while prioritizing large and obvious regions. The core prompt structure (simplified for clarity) is: 

Input: One RGB image + fixed vocabulary Goal: Return ONLY a JSON array of strings (no code block, no prose) with UP TO 8labels that correspond to the LARGEST and MOST OBVIOUS regions in the image. Selection rules: - Include only labels that are obvious and match with high confidence; - Use EXACT spelling from the vocabulary; - DO NOT invent new labels; - ... Vocabulary: [wall, floor, cabinet, ...] Output format example: ["wall", "floor", "table", ...] 

This simplified representation captures the essential prompt logic. The full implementation includes additional constraints to prevent hallucination and ensure deterministic JSON pars-ing.

Encoding into CLIP text space: As shown in the left panel of Fig. 4(c), the filtered class names are encoded via the CLIP text encoder. Following DenseCLIP [16], we apply a template ensemble strategy: each class name lj is wrapped into multiple contextualized prompts (e.g., “ a photo of a ... ” and “ an image of a ... ”), encoded via the CLIP text encoder Etext , and the resulting embeddings are averaged to produce tj . The full text embedding matrix is: 

T = [ t1, . . . , tN ] ∈ RN ×Ct , (3) where Ct = 512 is the CLIP text embedding dimension and 

N = |L| is the number of retrieved class names. The resulting matrix T serves as semantic guidance throughout the network, helping suppress irrelevant visual tokens and stabilizing cross-modal alignment. Although CLIP text embeddings are trained to align with CLIP image features, DTFormer does not require RGB-D segmentation features to lie in CLIP image space. In TSAM, visual features are projected into queries, while text embeddings are projected into keys and values; cross-modal attention is then computed in this attention space and optimized by the segmentation objective.

C. Network Architecture 

Fig. 4(a) presents the overall architecture of DTFormer, which adopts a hierarchical encoder-decoder structure built upon the DFormerv2 backbone [19]. The encoder ex-tracts multi-scale features at four stages with resolutions 

{h/ 4, h/ 8, h/ 16 , h/ 32 }. Stage 1 contains RGB-D blocks with geometry self-attention, while stages 2–4 extend to RGB-D-T blocks (Fig. 4(b)) by integrating TSA-E for text-guided feature extraction. The decoder applies TSA-D at each pyramid level before feature fusion. After TSA-D, features are upsampled to a common resolution and fused through a Ham-style decoder head [51]. A final segmentation head produces per-pixel pre-dictions. IV. E XPERIMENTS

E. Qualitative Results 

Fig. 6 presents qualitative comparisons between DTFormer and DFormerv2 baseline on NYU Depth V2. As shown, DT-Former produces improved segmentation across diverse indoor scenes, including bedrooms, offices, and dining rooms. The results demonstrate better handling of challenging structures such as furniture details. These improvements are consistent with the quantitative gains reported in Sec. IV-B. V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented DTFormer, a tri-modal framework that brings explicit semantic guidance to RGB-D segmentation through hierarchical text-visual-geometry alignment. Our key insight is that text guidance should be stage-adaptive: lightweight in early encoder stages to preserve geometric reasoning, and en-riched in decoder stages for robust semantic-geometric align-ment. To address the semantic ambiguity of fixed vocabularies, we leverage vision-language models to generate image-specific class names with intersection-based filtering, ensuring reliable and focused semantic priors.
