## ABSTRACT 

We introduce S ELF -S ALIENCY , a method for training Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to increase the alignment between their visual attention and the image re-gions mentioned in their reasoning. S ELF -S ALIENCY uses a grounding model to localize the objects mentioned in each reasoning step and treats the resulting areas as supervision for the model’s visual attention. Previous work on steering visual attention determines target image regions based solely on the image and question. In contrast, we show that conditioning the target regions on the model’s generated reasoning improves downstream performance. For proper evaluation, we build a unified, broad suite of 25 visual reasoning benchmarks, where we reproduce the results of previous methods. We find that S ELF -S ALIENCY significantly outper-forms both prior attention-steering methods and baselines that ground image-level text, achieving both a better average rank, and better mean score. Post-training analysis shows that the model primarily adapts its reasoning text to existing at-tention patterns, producing shorter steps that refer to larger regions. Nevertheless, when controlling for generated text, attention to grounded regions increases sig-nificantly across the relevant layer. Finally, we identify a consistent geometric bias in VLM visual attention toward the image border. However, our ablations show that S ELF -S ALIENCY ’s gains cannot be explained by simply aligning atten-tion with the center of the image, highlighting the importance of aligning visual attention with the regions mentioned in the model’s reasoning. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

When answering questions about images, humans shift their gaze across different image regions. Vision-language models (VLMs) exhibit an analogous mechanism during visual reasoning: gener-ated text tokens incorporate visual information by attending to image tokens. Surprisingly, however, the regions emphasized by VLMs differ substantially from those attended to by humans. Indeed, a growing body of work shows that visual attention often fails to focus on image regions that humans consider important for answering the question (Gong et al., 2024; Darcet et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025c). Numerous methods attempted to address this mismatch by post-training steering (Gong et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026). All these methods determine the target salient regions solely from the input image and question. By prescribing where the model should attend, they also constrain the reasoning paths available to the model. However, there is no reason to assume that these prescribed paths are optimal, or that other reasoning paths could not also lead to the correct answer. This motivates an unexplored alternative: deriving the target regions from the model’s own reasoning chain. To this end, we propose a post-training method, S ELF -S ALIENCY (Figure 1). During training, SELF -S ALIENCY uses a grounding model to localize the image regions that are referenced in each reasoning step. The trained model is rewarded for greater alignment between its visual attention and these grounded regions. At inference time, the trained model doesn’t utilize external models. 1arXiv:2610.05023v1 [cs.CV] 4 Oct 2026 Figure 1: S ELF -S ALIENCY is trained to increase the alignment between visual attention and the regions referenced in its reasoning chain, creating two complementary pressures: (1) generate claims that can be grounded in the image, and (2) attend to the regions those claims refer to. Inspecting recent literature, we find no broad consensus on evaluation benchmarks for visual rea-soning. A large number of benchmarks have been introduced in recent years, with individual papers typically reporting results on only a subset of them. To provide a more comprehensive evaluation, we identify the benchmarks used in recent work and construct a suite of 25 benchmarks. We evaluate S ELF -S ALIENCY on this broad suite and find that it substantially outperforms relevant baselines, with particularly strong gains on tasks that require attending to specific image regions. Surprisingly, these improvements are not limited to natural images. Although the grounding model was trained primarily on natural-image data, S ELF -S ALIENCY also improves performance on visual mathematical reasoning tasks. We analyze whether training primarily aligns the generated text with existing attention patterns or shifts attention toward the generated reasoning. S ELF -S ALIENCY produces shorter reasoning steps that refer to larger image regions. To isolate attention changes from this shift in text, we teacher-force the pre- and post-training models on the same reasoning chains. While grounded-region attention does not increase in the two heads used for the saliency reward, it increases significantly when averaged across all heads in the relevant layer. Finally, we analyze visual attention patterns across several VLM architectures and identify a con-sistent geometric bias: VLMs allocate disproportionately high attention to regions near the image border, whereas human annotations of task-relevant regions are more concentrated toward the im-age center. Motivated by this observation, we demonstrate that simply steering attention toward the image center does not improve downstream performance over the baselines, indicating that S ELF -SALIENCY ’s gains cannot be explained by a generic center bias. Finally, S ELF -S ALIENCY outper-forms a baseline that grounds image-level text, suggesting that grounding the model’s reasoning chain provides a more effective training signal. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Reasoning VLMs. Recent work has increasingly focused on training VLMs to perform explicit reasoning over images. Several studies investigate the construction of multimodal chain-of-thought data for this purpose (Yang et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026). Others modify the training procedure itself to improve reasoning behavior (Zhang et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025a). 2Training VLMs to relocate attention. Several previous studies have proposed methods for re-distributing visual attention. VAR (Kang et al., 2025a) and LASER (Yuan et al., 2026) transfer attention mass from sink tokens to non-sink tokens. VGA (Zhao et al., 2026) operates at inference time, steering attention toward image patches whose visual representations are more strongly asso-ciated with the objects referenced in the question. RAL (Li et al., 2026a) encourages the model’s attention distribution to move toward attention patterns of correct reasoning chains. Most closely related to our approach, several prior works steer attention toward content-dependent ground-truth regions. KLAL (Esmaeilkhani & Latecki, 2026) and Saliency-R1 (Gong et al., 2026) use human-annotated bounding boxes as supervision targets. EASE (Hu et al., 2026) prompts GPT to identify phrases that are important for answering the question, grounds these phrases using a grounding model, and treats the resulting regions as ground truth. To the best of our knowledge, SELF -S ALIENCY is the first method to derive grounded supervision directly from the reasoning chain generated by the policy being trained. 

Visual attention analysis. In recent years, several studies have investigated how visual attention is distributed across an image. Darcet et al. (2024) and Gong et al. (2024) argue that a large fraction of visual attention is assigned to outlier tokens with large norms. Kang et al. (2025a) define visual sink tokens as tokens that consistently receive high attention due to their intrinsic properties, rather than their relevance to the generated text. They further suggest that such sink behavior arises from large activations in specific dimensions of the tokens’ hidden states. Other studies show that LLaVA (Liu et al., 2024a) allocates a disproportionate amount of attention to the lower part of the image (Zhang et al., 2025c; Zhao et al., 2025). In Section 5, we extend this line of analysis to several VLMs and show that visual attention is consistently concentrated along the geometric border of the image. 

## 3 METHOD 

3.1 PRELIMINARIES 

Image Patches. Most modern VLMs begin image processing by dividing the image into a grid of disjoint patches. A vision transformer jointly processes the patches and outputs a contextualized representation. These visual representations are then passed through an adapter that maps them into visual tokens. Thus, each visual token retains a spatial correspondence to an original image patch. 

Attention Weights. When generating a new token, the self-attention mechanism computes a weight for each preceding token. These attention weights determine the contribution of each preceding token’s value vector to the output representation of the current token at that layer. Attention weights are therefore often used as a proxy for which tokens the model is focusing on. 3.2 SETTING 

To measure how well the model’s attention aligns with the image regions referenced in its reasoning chain, we define a saliency score function. We first decompose each reasoning chain into atomic reasoning steps and retain only those that describe observations about the image. 1 We compute the saliency score at the step level and apply it only to these observation steps. 3.3 SALIENCY SCORE

To apply our saliency score during training, we propose a post-training procedure based on reinforce-ment learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), using GRPO (Shao et al., 2024b). In this setting, the reward is computed deterministically from the model’s generated response. 43.5 HEAD SELECTION 

Following prior work (Kang et al., 2025b), we compute the saliency reward using only a small set of selected attention heads. To identify these heads, we run the base model (Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct; Bai et al., 2025) on the Visual-CoT dataset (Shao et al., 2024a) and compute the saliency reward Rsal (Section 3.4) for each sample and attention head. We then measure, for each head, the correlation between its saliency reward and model accuracy, and select the two heads with the highest correlation. 3 This procedure selects heads 28 and 31 in layer 22, out of 36 layers. This selection is consistent with the findings of Gandikota & Bau (2026), who show that the strongest alignment between attention and the image region being described occurs in layers 20–28. In par-ticular, their Figure 1B shows that, for Qwen3-8B-VL, layer-wise residual-stream steering alters the model’s spatial referent most strongly at layer 22. 

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 TRAINING 

To bootstrap the model’s reasoning capabilities we start by training the base model, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025) using supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the LLaVA-CoT (Xu et al., 2025) and Mulberry-SFT (Yao et al., 2025) datasets. We then start GRPO training from the resulting checkpoint on the Saliency-R1-8K (Gong et al., 2026) dataset, holding out 100 of its 8,080 samples, using group size of G = 8 . We set αf ormat = αdirect = αllm = 1 and αsal = 0 .4.4

Auxiliary models. For grounding we use Grounding-DINO (Liu et al., 2024b), and as the LLM judge needed for Rllm we use GPT-4o mini (OpenAI, 2024). 

Infrastructure. Training runs on a single node of eight H100 GPUs in bfloat16 under DeepSpeed ZeRO-3: six GPUs hold the policy, one serves generation with vLLM, and one hosts the grounding model. A full run takes approximately 42 seconds per optimizer step, or about 46 hours. 4.2 EVALUATION 

Benchmarks. Recent studies have introduced a large number of visual question answering (VQA) benchmarks (Kim et al., 2025). However, most papers evaluate on only a small subset, raising the possibility of benchmark-selection bias. For example, VGA (Zhao et al., 2026), Saliency-R1 (Gong et al., 2026), and EASE (Hu et al., 2026) evaluate on 3, 10, and 11 benchmarks, respectively, with only a single benchmark shared across all three. To mitigate this concern, we evaluate on all bench-marks used in recent work, together with several recently introduced benchmarks, yielding a com-prehensive suite of 25 benchmarks listed in Table 1. We evaluate with lmms-eval (Zhang et al., 2025b). 

Baselines. We first consider several baselines that do not explicitly intervene on visual attention: the vanilla Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct model, our coldstart checkpoint (the checkpoint after SFT we use to initialize GRPO), and a model trained using the same procedure as S ELF -S ALIENCY but without the saliency reward, i.e., using only the accuracy and format rewards ( No-Sal ). The latter tests whether the observed performance gains can be attributed simply to additional post-training rather than to the saliency objective itself. We also compare against methods from prior work that steer visual attention toward different image regions. ## 5 ATTENTION ANALYSIS AND ABLATIONS 

In this section, we analyze the visual attention patterns of commonly used VLMs to better understand the training signal provided by S ELF -S ALIENCY . Prior work has suggested that visual attention tends to concentrate on background regions (Gong et al., 2024; Darcet et al., 2024) or regions that are visually confusing or semantically related to the target object (Wang et al., 2026). 6 We propose a framing that does not contradict previous findings, but is more useful for our purposes: VLM attention is disproportionately concentrated along the geometric border of the image. 5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We use 1,800 (image, question) pairs from the Visual-CoT dataset (Shao et al., 2024a) to prompt four released instruction-tuned VLMs: Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025), InternVL3.5-8B (Wang et al., 2025b), GLM-4.1V-Thinking (Hong et al., 2025) and Nemotron-Omni (Deshmukh et al., 2026). 7 For every image we run a forward pass and record, at every layer and head, the attention each image patch receives, averaged over the generated tokens – and renormalized within the image. 8

Given a set of patches, we define its enrichment (En ) as the fraction of total visual attention assigned to the set, normalized by the fraction of image patches it contains. An enrichment of En = 1 

indicates no effect, while En > 1 and En < 1 indicate disproportionately high and low attention, respectively. We record two sets of patches: the border ring (all patches on the border of the image) and the center (all patches but the border ring). 5.2 RESULTS 

Table 4 shows enrichment results. Across all models, attention is more enriched along the border ring than in the center of the image. Moreover, in every model, the patch with the highest enrichment is located at a corner of the grid. Figure 4 visualizes the enrichment of each image patch for every model.

To isolate the contribution of different components of our method, we train two variants using the same procedure as S ELF -S ALIENCY , while changing only the source of the target bounding boxes. Motivated by the findings in Section 5.2, center rect uses a fixed rectangle centered in the image. If this variant performs comparably to S ELF -S ALIENCY , it would suggest that the gains primarily result from shifting attention away from the grid corner. In contrast, question boxes applies the grounding model to the input question rather than to the model’s reasoning steps, allowing us to test whether chain-level grounding provides additional benefit over grounding image-level text. We present the results in Table 5 and show a clear advantage for S ELF -S ALIENCY over the ablation variants. This finding indicates that grounding the model’s reasoning chain provides a meaningful signal for alignment with visual attention. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced S ELF -S ALIENCY , a post-training method for vision-language models. Rather than prescribing target regions from the input image and question alone, S ELF -S ALIENCY segments the model’s reasoning chain into steps, grounds the objects mentioned in each observation step, and rewards the model for attending to those regions during GRPO training. ## A FURTHER IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

A.1 REASONING STEPS SEGMENTATION AND CLASSIFICATION 

Given a reasoning chain c generated in response to a question q, we first decompose it into an ordered sequence of atomic reasoning steps, c = ⟨s1, . . . , s |c|⟩. Each step is itself a sequence of tokens, si = ⟨t1, . . . , t |si|⟩, and is assigned a functional label li.We define four functional categories for reasoning steps: plan , a forward-looking statement describ-ing an intended action (e.g., “let me check the left side of the image”); observe , a direct statement 14 about the visual content of the image (e.g., “there is a red car on the left”); deduce , a conclu-sion derived from previous reasoning steps rather than from newly introduced visual evidence (e.g., “therefore it is a traffic sign”); and none , a statement that does not contribute to the reasoning process (e.g., “the user asked where the cat is”). 

Segmentation. We segment the reasoning chain at sentence-final punctuation marks and line breaks, and discard fragments shorter than ten characters. 

Classification. We classify each reasoning step using an encoder-only model trained by distilling labels produced by a proprietary model. 9

After classification, we retain only steps labeled as observe , as these steps explicitly describe visual evidence from the image. A.2 TRAINING 

Coldstart. For the coldstart, we use LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) of rank 128 on all linear layers, learning rate 5 × 10 −5, and cosine schedule with 5% warmup, training for two epochs. 

GRPO training. We run GRPO (Shao et al., 2024b) for three epochs. Each step draws 6 prompts and samples G = 8 rollouts per prompt at temperature 1.0, for 48 completions per step (one com-pletion per device, gradient accumulation 8). Prompts are truncated at 2,048 tokens and completions at 1,024 , and images are resized so that the longer side is at most 512 pixels. We update only LoRA adapters of rank 16 (α = 32 , dropout 0.05 ) on the query and value projections, using AdamW at a learning rate of 1 × 10 −5 decayed linearly to zero with no warmup, no weight decay, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. Advantages are normalized by the standard deviation of the group’s rewards, and we use no KL penalty towards a reference policy ( β = 0 ), so the reward of Section 3.4 is the entire training signal. 

Judge model. As the LLM judge needed for Rllm we use GPT-4o mini (OpenAI, 2024) prompted with the question, the ground-truth answer and the model’s extracted answer span, and asked for a correctness rating on a 1–5 scale that we rescale to [0 , 1] .A.3 EVALUATION 

Evaluation protocol. We evaluate with lmms-eval (Zhang et al., 2025b), temperature 0, single beam with max new tokens = 4096 , repetition penalty = 1 .05 , batch size 1, in bfloat16. Images are processed at lmms-eval’s default budget of 1,605 ,632 pixels, raised to 3,211 ,264 for the three benchmarks whose subject is resolution (MME-RealWorld, HR-Bench 4K/8K). Benchmarks whose scoring requires answer extraction or an LLM judge use GPT-4o mini (OpenAI, 2024). 

Benchmark splits. We use the following splits for the evaluation benchmarks: soft localization for IllusionVQA, testmini vision only for MathVerse, testmini for MathVision, testmini for MathVista, 

standard for MMMUPro, test for Omnispatial, p3 for SalBench, img for ScienceQA, testmini for WeMath.

## D REIMPLEMENTING BASELINES WITHOUT A PUBLISHED CHECKPOINT 

In this section, we describe our reimplementation of baselines without a published checkpoint. D.1 VGA VGA (Zhao et al., 2026) is training-free, so our replication is an inference-time patch on Qwen3-VL rather than a retrained checkpoint. We keep the published hyperparameters – the guidance strength 

β, the top-K of the object-agnostic map, and the head-balancing scheme. Two points still required a decision, in each case because the original is backbone-specific or under-specified. We flag them in the following paragraphs. 

The layer window. The window over which guidance is applied is backbone-specific in the original, and the rule for choosing it – start where attention to the sink position begins to dominate – fixes 17 neither a quantity nor a threshold. On Qwen3-VL-8B its two natural readings disagree, selecting layer 3 or layer 7 against the published 4. Rather than choose, we swept five windows spanning both (start ∈ { 2, 4, 7}, end ∈ { 16 , 20 }, β ∈ { 0.2, 0.25 }) over 13 benchmarks at 100 documents each. The published window scores highest and the spread across all five is 0.005 , so we report VGA as published; the result is not an artefact of under-tuning. 

The object’s first token. The map reads the probability of the object’s first token at each visual position, but under BPE a word and its space-prefixed form are distinct tokens and it is not stated which is intended. We score the plain, space-prefixed and capitalised forms and keep the largest, which can only raise a real object above the floor. D.2 SALIENCY -R1 We reimplement Saliency-R1 (Gong et al., 2026) from the authors’ public repository 10 , changing as little as the backbone swap allows. Their released checkpoints are Qwen2.5-VL-3B and -7B; no Qwen3-VL model is released. 

Cold start. The cold-start stage described in Section 4.1 is theirs, simply on Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct instead of Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct. The Saliency-R1 row and all our own rows therefore begin from the same weights, and the comparison isolates the RL stage. 

Reward. Their saliency map is computed as released: for each rollout, the attention of the answer tokens to the reasoning span is composed with the reasoning span’s attention to the image tokens, weighted by the corresponding value vectors, projected through the output projection and the un-embedding, summed over answer tokens, rectified, and reshaped onto the merged patch grid. The reward is the fraction of that map’s mass falling inside the question-level box shipped with Saliency-R1-8K, and zero for completions that fail their format check. Our only edit to this code is that rather than retaining per-step attention throughout sampling, we sample first and recover the same attention rows from a single teacher-forced pass over the sampled sequence. Under causal attention the two are algebraically identical, and the second fits 1,024-token completions in memory. 

GRPO configuration. We keep their published command: the same corpus and the same 100-sample held-out split (seed 42), their system prompt, learning rate 1 × 10 −5, three epochs, G = 8 

rollouts at temperature 1.0, LoRA on the query and value projections at the library defaults their command leaves unset (rank 16, α = 32 , dropout 0.05 ), no KL penalty, and their global batch of 64 completions per optimizer step – redistributed as one completion per device with gradient accumulation 8 over eight GPUs, rather than their four and two, for memory. All eight GPUs train, giving 8 prompts per step and 2,991 optimizer steps for three epochs rather than the 3,990 

of Section 4.1. We deviate from their command in three places. First, prompts and completions are capped at 2,048 and 1,024 tokens instead of 1,024 and 512 , matching our other runs; the cap was rarely active, with mean completion length between 162 and 247 tokens and fewer than 0.5% 

of rollouts reaching it. Second, we add the same exact-match reward Rdirect that every other arm receives, so the reward here is Rformat + Rdirect + Rllm + RS-R1 sal and this arm differs from ours in the attention term alone; we leave their weight of 1 on RS-R1 sal rather than retuning it to our αsal . Third, training-time sampling uses no repetition penalty, where their command sets 1.05 . We use the same correctness judge they use, GPT-4o-mini with their prompt.
