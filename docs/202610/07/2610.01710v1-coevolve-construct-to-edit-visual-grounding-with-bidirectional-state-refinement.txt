ABSTRACT 

Visual grounding localizes an object described by language with a bounding box. Most multimodal grounding models compress target identification, spatial reason-ing, and boundary estimation into one terminal prediction. Free-form rationales make reasoning linguistically explicit but do not necessarily expose measurable, editable spatial states. Intermediate localization errors are therefore difficult to diagnose and correct, allowing incorrect region choices and imprecise boundaries to persist in the final box. We introduce CoEvolve , a construct-to-edit frame-work that separates grounding into explicit state construction and state editing. Region-Evolution Reinforcement (RER) organizes grounding analysis into a pro-gressive semantic–spatial trajectory, with each reasoning step committing to an explicit candidate region. Bidirectional Denoising Refiner (BDR) treats the rea-soning text as fixed semantic context and refines the trajectory’s coordinate fields through bidirectional same-position reconstruction. Geometry- and behavior-level objectives provide target geometry and edit-preference signals for consolidating reliable candidates, preserving accurate inputs, or correcting toward annotations. Evaluations cover natural-image and remote-sensing grounding. With a 9B back-bone, CoEvolve rivals models up to 241B parameters in grounding accuracy. Under controlled corruption, a single BDR pass improves mean box overlap by over 27 percentage points, demonstrating strong recovery from substantial lo-calization errors. State-source comparisons further support the complementar-ity of explicit state construction and source-matched editing. The project is at 

https://sundongwei.github.io/CoEvolve_Project/ .

1 INTRODUCTION 

Visual grounding, or referring expression comprehension, localizes a language-described object with a bounding box. The field has progressed from proposal-based and modular grounding (Kazemzadeh et al., 2014; Mao et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2016; 2018; Zhang et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2019b; Yang et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019a) to end-to-end Transformers and grounded pre-training (Deng et al., 2021; Kamath et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022; Yan et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023), and more recently to multimodal large language models (MLLMs) that unify language understanding and coordinate generation (You et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a;b; Bai et al., 2025a;b; Wang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024). Our contributions are: • We introduce CoEvolve and its RER component to expose intermediate localization deci-sions as a parseable semantic–spatial trajectory, providing an explicit state for inspection and correction. • We develop BDR to edit the coordinate fields of this state through position-aligned same-position reconstruction. Geometry- and behavior-level objectives jointly provide target geometry and edit-preference signals for state refinement. • We demonstrate strong target localization across natural-image and remote-sensing bench-marks, together with substantial correction gains under controlled box corruption.

An outcome-only box reward does not directly assess whether intermediate region states make target-directed progress before the terminal prediction. RER therefore adapts an autoregressive policy πθ (y | I, q ) to construct a measurable localization state. A valid response contains one 

<refine > block with 3–5 steps and one <answer > block. We denote its parsed sequence by 

τ (y), where each pair (rk, bk) associates a reasoning step with an explicit region and bans is the ter-minal answer. Unlike regions that identify visual evidence in grounded reasoning (Fan et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026), each RER box is a candidate location for the referred target, and the ordered se-quence becomes BDR’s editable state. We use semantic–spatial state to denote this division of roles: free-form text supplies semantic context, while each step exposes a spatial commitment evaluated by trajectory-level rewards. These rewards assess the spatial commitments rather than the semantic correctness of the free-form text itself. For each sampled response, RER assigns the sequence-level reward 

RRER (y, b∗) = 0 .5Rfmt (y) + 0 .5Rstep (y) + Rprog (y, b∗) + Rbox (y, b∗). (6) 

Rfmt checks the outer tagged structure and provides graded feedback for partially valid responses. 

Rstep checks whether each refinement step ends in a parseable box; it does not score localization accuracy. When 3–5 valid non-empty regions are available, Rprog evaluates target-directed spatial 4progress using overlap, center, coverage, and final-consistency signals. Rbox parses and evaluates the terminal answer by its overlap and shape agreement with b∗, and assigns the prescribed negative reward when the answer is missing, repeated, malformed, or invalid. The appendix specifies the piecewise definitions and coefficients of these terms. We sample eight responses per input and use Eq. (6) in the GRPO objective introduced in Section 2. The annotated terminal box evaluates the generated regions, but RER requires no manually labeled reasoning text or intermediate box trajectory. It therefore derives trajectory-level spatial rewards from the terminal box annotation and organizes the search process into a structured state whose coordinate fields BDR can refine. 3.3 BIDIRECTIONAL DENOISING REFINER 

Editable localization states. BDR refines complete RER responses, using their fixed reasoning text as semantic context and editing the coordinate fields of historical Steps, the last Step, and the Answer. Geometry-level and behavior-level objectives supervise target coordinates and edit decisions, while auxiliary reconstruction and entropy terms preserve the response and regularize confidence. For states whose RER Answer has IoU above 0.5, we sample ξ ∼ Bernoulli(0 .3) ; for all other states, we set ξ = 0 . Given x(0) = Tok( y), we use xin = x(0) when ξ = 0 and xin =Tok( Q(y, b∗)) otherwise, where Q replaces selected boxes with valid geometric perturbations.

4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate CoEvolve on standard referring-expression grounding, localization under stricter IoU criteria, controlled box correction, component ablation, and remote-sensing grounding. Unless oth-erwise noted, aggregate evaluation metrics are reported as percentages. 64.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Datasets and Evaluation. For natural-image grounding, we train CoEvolve only on the Ref-COCOg training set and evaluate all eight RefCOCO-family splits (57,457 examples) without further fine-tuning (Mao et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2016). RefCOCOg measures in-dataset performance, while RefCOCO and RefCOCO+ provide cross-dataset evaluation under RefCOCOg-only training. We report split-wise Acc@0 .5 and their arithmetic mean (Overall). Stricter-IoU, controlled-correction, and ablation experiments use five test splits comprising 30,969 examples. Unless stated otherwise, their aggregate metrics are computed jointly over all examples rather than by averaging the five split-level scores. Remote-sensing grounding is evaluated on DIOR-RSVG (Zhan et al., 2023). 4.2 STANDARD REF COCO-F AMILY GROUNDING

CoEvolve reformulates visual grounding as a construct-to-edit process: RER externalizes measur-able region commitments, and BDR refines their coordinates through bidirectional same-position reconstruction with geometry-level and behavior-level supervision. Experiments across natural im-ages, controlled perturbations, and remote-sensing scenes support this decomposition. The state-source comparison further shows that refinement depends not only on initial localization quality, but also on how the editable state is constructed and how its editor is trained.

This appendix gives the complete mathematical specification of RER and BDR. We use the same notation as in the main paper and make explicit which quantities are used only to construct training targets and which quantities are visible to the model. B.1 NOTATION AND THE RER–BDR I NTERFACE 

For an image–expression pair (I, q ), a box is represented in the normalized integer coordinate system by b = [ x1, y 1, x 2, y 2] ∈ [0 , 1000] 4, with x1 < x 2 and y1 < y 2. The annotated box is b∗. RER produces a response

RER and BDR are optimized sequentially and communicate only through this generated state. B.2 SEQUENTIAL TRAINING AND BDR D ATASET CONSTRUCTION 

CoEvolve is trained sequentially rather than end to end. We first optimize RER on the RefCOCOg training set using the rewards defined below. After RER training is complete, its adapter is frozen and used to generate one complete localization response for each RefCOCOg training expression. Each saved record pairs the original image and referring expression with the complete RER assistant response and its annotated box. The generated RER responses are then used to construct the BDR training set. BDR initializes a separate adapter on the frozen Qwen3.5-9B backbone; it is not initialized from the RER adapter. At loading time, auxiliary <think > content is removed, every coordinate is zero-padded to four digits, and the remaining <refine > and <answer > sequence is used as the editable input state. The unmodified records retain the natural errors produced by RER. Eligi-ble high-overlap states are additionally augmented with synchronized geometric perturbations, as detailed in Section B.4, so BDR observes both the natural RER error distribution and deliberately corrupted anchors. The annotation is retained only for target construction and loss computation and is never inserted into the model input. B.3 REGION -E VOLUTION REINFORCEMENT

B.5 INFERENCE PROCEDURE 

At inference, RER first generates the complete localization state. BDR then performs a deterministic same-position pass on the current state. After each pass, only the editable coordinate positions are updated; the surrounding reasoning text and response structure are retained. The response is then detokenized and tokenized again, coordinate masks are rebuilt, and the updated state becomes the next-pass input. We use two BDR passes for all standard natural-image and remote-sensing eval-uations, providing a consistent refinement budget across settings. Unparseable final <answer >

boxes remain in the evaluation denominator and receive zero IoU. B.6 PROMPT AND EVALUATION PROTOCOLS

E LIMITATIONS 

Imperfect preserve-or-edit decisions. BDR improves correction behavior on average but does not guarantee an improvement for every example. In controlled correction, refinement degrades 

15 .50% of the anchors, compared with 11 .42% for Qwen3.5-9B. Quality-gated targets encourage preservation of accurate inputs, but the learned preserve-or-edit decision remains imperfect for a residual subset. Calibrated edit acceptance, uncertainty-aware refinement, or adaptive stopping could retain the current state when a further update is unlikely to help. 

State–editor pairing and broader transfer. The source-matched comparison shows that RER →BDR achieves stronger final refinement than the separately trained native-output pipeline. This result motivates further study of how state representations interact with their corresponding editors, particularly across upstream model families and response formats. 

Coordinate-centric editing. BDR treats reasoning text as fixed semantic context and edits the coordinate fields of the localization state. This design keeps the semantic context fixed during position-aligned refinement, but semantic errors that cannot be resolved through coordinate correc-tion remain outside the current editor’s scope. The current objective also does not explicitly enforce semantic-role consistency between an earlier reasoning step and its edited historical box. Extending bidirectional editing toward jointly editable semantic–spatial states is a promising direction. 30
