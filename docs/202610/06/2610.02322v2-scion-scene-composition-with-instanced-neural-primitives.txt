# SCION: Scene Composition with Instanced Neural Primitives

## Abstract 

Real-world scenes are compositional: bricks, blades of grass, pebbles, and tree leaves recur across human-built and natural environments. Existing neural scene representations model these elements independently. Most 3D Gaussian Splatting and follow-up abstraction and compression methods treat each element as unique, fitting millions of independent Gaussians per scene. Prior methods like Splat and Replace fit template objects, but they require mostly manual selection of repeated elements. As a result, these representations store redundant parameters and provide weak manipulation handles for downstream tasks. We introduce SCION, a hier-archical compositional scene representation that replaces independent Gaussians with a compact vocabulary of reusable primitives and lightweight world-space instances that place transformed copies throughout the scene. We fit this represen-tation to multi-view captures via a joint optimization over discrete and continuous scene parameters, combining two-level densification over splats and instances with an adversarial loss that preserves detail across shared primitives. The recovered structure yields a compact, controllable representation while maintaining high quality even at 1.2 MB. SCION achieves rate-distortion favorable to existing Gaussian compression methods, and it enables instance-level scene editing and animation without retraining. Our results show that neural scene representations need not memorize scenes as independent primitives; they can discover reusable parts. Project webpage: https://light.princeton.edu/SCION 

## 1 Introduction 

The natural and built world is full of repetition. A meadow is a few species of grass blade copied a million times; a brick wall is one brick under different poses; a forest canopy is a handful of leaf shapes scattered across branches. Traditionally, in computer graphics, we therefore do not manually handcraft every single leaf individually, but just a handful of templates which we then instantiate using a particle system or array [Prusinkiewicz and Lindenmayer, 1990, Stava et al., 2014]. This does more than save labor: the resulting scenes are much easier to animate, are faster to edit, and require significantly less memory. 3D reconstruction deals with the inverse problem: going from images to a 3D representation. Neural radiance fields and related neural graphics primitives shifted reconstruction toward photorealistic view synthesis [Mildenhall et al., 2020, Müller et al., 2022]. However, because these scenes are represented as neural fields rather than physical structures, they are hard to integrate into graphics workflows. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [Kerbl et al., 2023] in turn uses an explicit representation, which makes rendering and integration into other tools easier and more efficient. Yet 3DGS fits millions of independent Gaussians per scene, each carrying its own position, covariance, opacity, and color. The reconstructions are accurate but they are also large, and structureless : nothing in the representation tells us when the ten thousand splats covering a lawn are instances of the same few blades.

Lack of structure limits the downstream usefulness of these scenes in two ways. First, it makes edits hard to express: an intuitive operation such as placing grass on a surface requires manually instantiating and tuning thousands of individual splats, instead of inserting a few reusable primitives. Second, it makes animation hard: the user must deform an unstructured collection of Gaussians instead of transforming coherent objects. Semantic segmentation can isolate large objects, but it does not recover the fine-grained repeated structure of leaves, grass, or other repetitive elements. Recovering structure requires a representation that discovers repetition. Neural rendering has largely left this problem untouched. Prior codebook-style methods target compression [Takikawa et al., 2022, Lee et al., 2024, Durvasula et al., 2025]: they share parameters across distinct splats rather than building shared primitives. They identify repetition in parameters, not in geometry. Explicit repetition has mostly appeared in analysis or exemplar pipelines. Regularity detection ana-lyzes input point clouds or meshes to discover similarity-group repetitions, rather than reconstructing them from images [Pauly et al., 2008]. The closest 3DGS work in this area, Splat and Replace, starts from an already trained 3DGS scene and relies on human-guided instance selection [Violante et al., 2025]. This makes it suitable for larger annotated objects, but difficult to scale to dense fine-grained repetitions. Identifying repetition creates two optimization challenges: choosing the unit of reuse, and tolerating variation across reused elements. A scene element can be represented as one instance of a large primitive containing many splats, or as many instances of a smaller primitive with fewer splats; the latter uses parameters more efficiently and yields a more structured representation, but is harder to optimize. This is a minimum description length problem. At the same time, real instances of the same element are not identical, so direct pixel-wise sharing drives templates toward blurry averages and erases high-frequency detail. It also means that there is no optimal solution to our minimum description length problem. We introduce SCION, which recovers a scene’s primitive decomposition end-to-end from multi-view captures (Fig. 1). SCION represents a scene as a small library C = {ck}Kk=1 of reusable primitives, each being a bundle of 3D Gaussians in a local frame, and a set of instances: lightweight world-space placements with pose (t, R), scale σ, opacity α, and assignment ai ∈ { 1, . . . , K }. The representation scales primarily with the number of sparse lightweight instances N rather than the number of primitives K or splats S. This leads to better rate–distortion tradeoffs. The factorization into primitives furthermore gives the scene direct editing and animation handles. Recovering this factorization is challenging: each primitive aggregates gradients from all referencing instances, and averaged reconstruction losses (e.g., L1) drive templates toward blurry means, erasing high-frequency detail. We address this with primitive-local splat birth and relocation, world-space instance pruning, cloning, and subdivision, together with an adversarial patch loss that preserves per-instance sharpness while still enabling shared structure. We make the following contributions in this work • We introduce SCION, a hierarchical representation learning method for compositional scene reconstruction that replaces independent Gaussians with a reusable vocabulary of primitives and lightweight transformed instances. The resulting 3D scenes are compact, structured, and directly manipulable. • We learn repetitions with new densification strategies for primitive-local splats and world-space instances, and integrate them in a joint optimization procedure that preserves sharpness across instance aggregation. • We demonstrate that the recovered structure has favorable rate–distortion tradeoffs over Gaussian compression methods at matched storage, while additionally enabling instance-level editing and animation without retraining.

Neural rendering & compression. 3D Gaussian Splatting [Kerbl et al., 2023] represents scenes with millions of independently parameterized anisotropic elements. Many variants improve this representation by modifying underlying geometry, optimization, or organization, e.g., surface-aligned disks or surfels [Huang et al., 2024a, Dai et al., 2024], mesh-attached Gaussians [Guédon and Lepetit, 2024], hierarchical levels of detail [Kerbl et al., 2024], or sampling-based densification and pruning [Kheradmand et al., 2024, Kim et al., 2025, Durvasula et al., 2025]. However, these methods largely preserve the per-splat parameterization. A large body of work compresses 3DGS by improving parameter encoding, entropy modeling, quantization, or predictive coding while keeping the underlying splat representation fixed [Lu et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2024, Zhan et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2025a, 2024, Xu et al., 2026, Chen et al., 2026, Liu et al., 2025b]. Other approaches modify the representation more directly through codebooks, residual quantization, distilled appearance, 2D projections, or alternative primitive elements such as textured surfels and billboards [Niedermayr et al., 2024, Lee et al., 2024, 2025, Fan et al., 2024, Morgenstern et al., 2025, Rong et al., 2025, Svitov et al., 2025]. Related compression ideas also appear in NeRFs, neural fields, point clouds, and 3D-aware generative models, where shared networks, grids, latent codebooks, or local decoders reduce storage [Mildenhall et al., 2020, Müller et al., 2022, Tang et al., 2026, Watanabe and Umetani, 2026, Ma et al., 2021, Van Den Oord et al., 2017, Takikawa et al., 2022, You and Gao, 2021, Li et al., 2023, Schwarz et al., 2020, Niemeyer and Geiger, 2021]. Across these methods, the atomic unit is still typically an independently stored primitive, feature, or latent code. In contrast, SCION reduces the number of independent parameters by sharing concrete geometric primitives across many instances through a discrete vocabulary C, improving compression while preserving perceived quality across scenes. 

Instance and pattern recognition. Recovering repeated structure has a long history, but most previous methods assume existing geometry, manual inputs, or strong structural priors. Classical regularity detection fits similarity-group lattices and representative elements to point clouds or meshes [Pauly et al., 2008], while inverse procedural modeling fits authored grammars to curated exemplars such as polygonal tree scans [Stava et al., 2014] or pre-segmented part hierarchies [Li et al., 2017]. Splat and Replace is closer to ours in using 3DGS, but starts from a trained scene and human-guided instance selection, then registers and fuses object-scale repeats post hoc [Violante et al., 2025]. Gaussian splat instantiation has also been explored for tiling, but not reconstruction [Zeng et al., 2025]. Recent multi-object reconstruction aggregates unaligned views of repeated objects, but still requires per-object segmentation [Wu et al., 2026]. Slot-based methods discover compositional radiance fields without masks [Yu et al., 2022]. Inverse-rendering approaches decompose scenes into geometry, materials, and lighting at urban scale [Wang et al., 2023]. However, both operate at object-level granularity. Feature uplifting transfers 2D foundation features into 3D for grouping [Marrie et al., 3Template Template Frequency Map Assembled Template Fully Assembled Render x 54 x 86 x 98 x 32 (a) (b) (c) (d)

Category-specific pipelines further restrict scope to a single class, such as botanical trees [Li et al., 2021]. In contrast, SCION learns a primitive vocabulary C and discrete instance assignments end-to-end from multi-view images. It requires no grammar, exemplar, or category prior, and as such, generalizes to any scene with repetitive structures in the wild. 

Controlling neural representations. Recent work adds control to 3DGS scenes by augmenting independently parameterized splats. Editing methods support brush- or patch-based authoring with diffusion inpainting [Pandey et al., 2025], test-time latent editors [Zheng et al., 2025b], or planar-prior anchoring for cleaner edits in textureless regions [Tibenský et al., 2025]. For motion, graph-based hierarchies infer parent–child relations for ARAP-style deformation [Zheng et al., 2025a], while dynamic 3DGS methods deform canonical Gaussians with MLP or HexPlane-style fields [Yang et al., 2024, Wu et al., 2024], but are typically not directly editable. Other controllable methods drive Gaussians using sparse control graphs, rigged face meshes, skinning, tracking, modal bases, or motion priors distilled from diffusion and spectral models [Huang et al., 2024b, Qian et al., 2024, Wimmer et al., 2025, Li et al., 2024]. These methods are often object-centric and add control on top of independent splats, rather than producing shared structures that are naturally animatable. SCION does not introduce a dedicated editing or animation system. Instead, its instance-based representation enables both implicitly. Edits or motions applied to a primitive propagate to all associated instances without per-splat handles. This is particularly effective in highly repetitive regimes, such as foliage, masonry, or tiled surfaces, where object-centric control is ill-defined. 

## 3 Learning the scene composition 

SCION learns a factorized Gaussian scene representation from posed training images. The scene is stored as a reusable vocabulary of local primitives and a larger set of world-space instances: each instance selects one primitive and places a transformed copy into the scene. We jointly optimize the shared primitive parameters and the instance placements with both image and adversarial supervision, as well as a new vocabulary-aware density optimization.

## 4 Experiments 

We evaluate SCION as a compact and meaningful scene representation. We trade per-pixel fidelity for reusable structures with direct manipulation handles. We showcase the validity of our method in two ways: quantitative evaluation of reconstruction quality under matched storage budgets, as well as qualitative evaluation, showcasing the meaningfulness with downstream applications. 

4.1 Evaluation protocol 

We evaluate on the Mip-NeRF 360 [Barron et al., 2022], Tanks & Temples [Knapitsch et al., 2017], Deep Blending [Hedman et al., 2018], and Synthetic-NeRF [Mildenhall et al., 2020] benchmarks using the standard training/test camera splits. Unless otherwise noted, SCION is trained for 30k iterations. We report PSNR, SSIM [Wang et al., 2004], LPIPS [Zhang et al., 2018], and on-disk model size in MB. Full per-benchmark results are deferred to Tab. S1 in the supplementary material.

4.3 Ablations 

We show the impact of the different components of our learning objective both quantitatively in Tab. 2 and qualitatively in Fig. 4. For the starting point, we disable LPIPS and adversarial loss, as well as disable densification control for instances. While it still uses a shared representation, densification only occurs on splat-level. We add the additional components of our methods cumulatively. We observe a perceptual vs. reconstruction trade-off rather than a strict gain: the components improve LPIPS and qualitative results at a small cost in PSNR and SSIM. The biggest qualitative improvement is introduced by the adversarial training (see Fig. 4). It is worth pointing out that the performance delta on LPIPS is larger by adding adversarial training than by adding an LPIPS loss - the very metric we are comparing against. This suggests that reference-based loss terms alone lead to a local optimization minimum. We share additional results on the impact of the number of primitives and initial splats per primitive in Tab. S2. 

4.4 Editability and animation Editability. The vocabulary representation exposes edit handles that are absent from a flat set of independent splats. We implement an editor that lets users add, remove, or replace recovered primitives through their instances. Because the initial vocabulary assignment carries instance-level DINO features, users can retrieve primitives with text queries and insert additional instances into the scene. Additional details and examples appear in Sec. S1.6 and the supplementary video. 

Animation. Animation benefits from the same factorization. A standard 3DGS scene must displace independent Gaussians; therefore, scene elements represented by many unrelated splats cannot move coherently. ## 5 Conclusion 

SCION reconstructs a scene as a compact vocabulary of reusable primitives together with lightweight world-space instances that place transformed copies throughout the scene. The central challenge is fitting this shared, partly discrete structure directly from multi-view images: each primitive receives supervision through many placed copies, while each instance must select and position a template. We make this optimization practical with vocabulary-scoped splat birth and relocation, world-space instance pruning, cloning, and subdivision, detached primitive/instance optimization, and adversarial patch supervision that preserves detail under sharing. The resulting representation is compact, structured, editable, and animatable. Our experiments show that this structure can reduce storage by orders of magnitude and expose manipulation handles that flat Gaussian fields lack, while making the expected tradeoff in image-space metrics. 

Limitations. SCION is most appropriate when the captured scene contains real repetition. In scenes dominated by unique geometry or strong per-instance variation, sharing becomes a poor prior: the method either underfits or spends capacity on effectively one-off templates. The current hard primitive assignment ai ∈ { 1, . . . , K } is fixed during reconstruction, which is stable but prevents gradients from moving an instance between plausible primitives. Training is also more expensive than vanilla 3DGS and many other methods because of the two-pass optimization, discriminator and LPIPS loss, as well as the additional density-control. 

Outlook. Direct extensions include soft or learned assignments, multi-class vocabularies for het-erogeneous scenes, and generative models over instance pose, scale, opacity, and assignment. More broadly, compositional vocabularies are a natural target for neural reconstruction: structured enough to edit and animate, compact enough to ship, and expressive enough to fit real captures. SCION is a step toward making recovered repeated parts a first-class output, rather than an afterthought imposed on an unstructured Gaussian field. 9References
